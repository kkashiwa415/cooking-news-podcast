Computing just hit a wall, and it's about to get way more expensive than anyone thought.

For years, the story was simple: bigger models, more power, better results. We've pushed silicon to its limits with staggering amounts of computing resources focused on training the next generation of AI systems. But here's what's getting less attention—it's not just the power bill that's skyrocketing. Every time an AI model runs and generates a response, it leaves behind mountains of data that have to be stored somewhere. And that storage bill is becoming the hidden cost nobody's talking about. 

Companies are generating billions of daily prompts, images, and videos, and all that back-end data needs to live somewhere. The infrastructure required to handle this data retention is massive and growing faster than the compute itself. This matters because it fundamentally changes the economics of AI deployment. When enterprises move from experimentation into everyday operations, they're discovering that the real expense isn't just running the models—it's keeping all that inference data on file. What to watch for is how companies start factoring data storage into their AI budgeting and whether we see new infrastructure players emerge to handle this side of the problem.

Now, there's also a broader question about how AI actually gets deployed in real businesses. Context-aware AI is becoming the frontier, and it's a completely different challenge than building a powerful model. Enterprise AI can generate recommendations, automate tasks, and help organizations make decisions faster. But here's the catch—without understanding how a business actually operates, even the most powerful models will miss critical constraints and relationships that determine whether a decision is actually the right one. A model might suggest something technically perfect that violates company policy or creates a bottleneck somewhere else in operations. This affects every organization trying to move AI from the lab into production. The real value isn't just in the model's capability; it's in whether the model understands your specific business context. Companies that figure out how to embed that contextual knowledge into their AI systems will have a significant advantage over those trying to force generic models into their workflows.

Speaking of computing and what comes next, we're living through a moment where the old limits of computing are starting to feel arbitrary. Decades ago, a physicist named Richard Feynman asked a question that still resonates: how far can we actually push computing? The answer has always been "further." We've miniaturized circuits to scales that seemed impossible, squeezed more transistors onto chips than anyone thought feasible, and now we're asking whether we can go even smaller. The fundamental question is whether the physics and economics of computing allow for the next leap forward. There's technological excitement about what becomes possible with fundamentally new approaches—quantum systems, optical computing, novel architectures that don't rely on traditional semiconductors. But it's also a moment of reckoning because we're hitting thermodynamic limits and manufacturing constraints that suggest we can't just keep doing what we've been doing. Watch for breakthroughs in novel computing paradigms over the next couple of years, because whoever cracks the next architectural leap will define computing for the next several decades.

In other news, the climate is sending distress signals from an unexpected direction. Permafrost—that layer of frozen soil that covers vast parts of the Arctic and Siberia—is thawing at an accelerating rate, and it's becoming a major driver of climate change that most people barely understand. Permafrost is frozen ground that stays frozen year-round, and it's incredibly carbon-rich because it contains layers of organic matter that's been locked away for thousands of years. When it thaws, microbes start breaking down that organic material, releasing methane and carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. This creates a vicious feedback loop: warming releases more greenhouse gases, which causes more warming, which causes more permafrost to thaw. The scale is enormous. We're talking about trillions of tons of carbon stored in permafrost, and as Arctic temperatures rise much faster than the global average, we're seeing rapid thawing in areas that have been frozen for millennia. This affects climate projections worldwide because it's one of the most significant tipping points in the climate system. The thawing permafrost in Alaska, Canada, Russia, and Siberia will continue accelerating, and this could add significantly to cumulative greenhouse gas emissions over the coming decades. It's not something we can directly control through carbon reduction alone—it's already happening—but it fundamentally changes what we need to do to stabilize the climate.
