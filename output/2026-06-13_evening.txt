Andrew Yang just identified what he thinks is the next trillion-dollar startup opportunity, and it's hiding in your monthly bills.

Yang's been analyzing where Americans are getting absolutely gouged on everyday costs. Housing, food, wireless service, insurance premiums—the list goes on. His thesis is simple but powerful: the next generation of huge exits won't come from flashy consumer apps or cutting-edge infrastructure. They'll come from startups that systematically undercut the bloated industries that have had a stranglehold on household budgets for decades. If someone can genuinely lower the cost of living in meaningful ways—whether that's through smarter logistics, better pricing models, or just cutting out middlemen—they're sitting on enormous venture potential. That resonates right now because Americans are feeling the squeeze harder than ever. Every percentage point you can shave off a family's annual expenses becomes a massive market if you can do it at scale. Watch for entrepreneurs pivoting toward this angle over the next year or so.

Now, here's something important that's flying under the radar for most investors. The question of how the Federal Reserve will tighten monetary policy without raising interest rates sounds like an oxymoron, but it's actually what Chair Warsh is preparing to do. The mechanics involve things like reducing the Fed's balance sheet more aggressively, tightening reserve requirements for banks, and using reverse repos to drain liquidity from the system. The idea is to restrain credit growth and make financial conditions tighter without pulling the lever on the fed funds rate. Why would they do this? Interest rates at zero are politically radioactive right now, but the Fed still has tools to slow the economy if growth overheats or inflation creeps back up. The trick is that these tools are less visible and less politically contentious than simply raising rates. For investors, this matters because the transmission mechanism of monetary policy could work differently than you're used to. If tightening happens through balance sheet moves rather than rate increases, certain sectors and asset classes will be affected differently. Growth stocks might hold up better in a higher-rate scenario than in a scenario where liquidity just quietly evaporates through Fed action. It's worth thinking about how your portfolio would behave in that environment.
