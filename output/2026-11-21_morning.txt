# MORNING EDITION — SATURDAY, NOVEMBER 21, 2026

There's a new threat in the AI world that has nothing to do with rogue algorithms — it's the people behind them who are getting trapped.

Security researchers are sounding the alarm about a sophisticated honeypot scheme targeting technologists and engineers across the artificial intelligence space. These aren't your typical phishing scams. What makes this particular threat so dangerous is that it exploits the very thing that drives ambitious people in tech — opportunity, connection, and the possibility of building something world-changing. Attackers are creating entirely believable personas, often posing as investors, collaborators, or fellow researchers in the AI field. They build relationships over weeks or months, establishing trust, before eventually steering conversations toward extracting sensitive information or access to proprietary systems. The scheme works because these targets are accustomed to rapid relationship-building in the startup world and often have access to deeply valuable intellectual property. What experts are warning right now is that this vulnerability cuts across the entire AI industry — from researchers at major labs to engineers at smaller startups to venture capitalists reviewing new projects. Everyone in the space appears susceptible. The concern matters because if attackers successfully compromise an AI researcher or engineer, they don't just gain access to individual credentials — they potentially unlock entire research programs, training datasets, or early versions of breakthrough systems. The outlook here is sobering. As AI becomes increasingly central to competition between companies and nations, these social engineering attacks are likely to become more sophisticated and better-resourced. Organizations in the space need to implement stronger verification protocols for new connections, especially those coming through professional networks or social media.

Turning to the world of digital assets, there's a brewing regulatory showdown in New York that could significantly reshape the cryptocurrency trading landscape. The prediction market platform Kalshi is racing to get ahead of a potential enforcement order from New York regulators that could effectively shut down its operations. The company has been operating in something of a legal gray area, offering prediction markets on everything from election outcomes to economic indicators. New York's Department of Financial Services has been scrutinizing whether Kalshi needs additional licensing to operate within the state, and sources close to the situation suggest an order could be imminent. Kalshi serves retail traders and institutions across the country and has become one of the more visible platforms in the prediction market space. The timing here is critical because if New York issues a cease-and-desist, other states could follow, and the entire prediction market industry would face similar questions. Kalshi's scramble right now appears focused on either reaching a negotiated settlement with regulators or finding a technical solution that keeps the platform compliant with New York law while maintaining its core functionality. What to watch is whether this becomes a template for how states regulate the broader crypto and digital assets ecosystem, or whether Kalshi successfully negotiates an exemption that sets a precedent for other platforms.

In other tech news, there's a significant convergence happening between venture capital and energy innovation. Spotify founder Daniel Ek is backing a fusion power startup called Europa Fusion, which was founded by an AI researcher. The company is working on what could be a game-changing approach to commercial fusion energy — the kind of clean, abundant power that could fundamentally transform how data centers and AI training facilities operate. This matters because the AI industry's demand for electricity is becoming one of the biggest constraints on growth. Training massive language models and running inference at scale requires enormous amounts of power, and traditional energy sources either can't scale fast enough or don't align with corporate sustainability goals. Europa Fusion is developing technology aimed at making fusion reactors smaller and more practical for commercial deployment than previous attempts. By backing this venture, Ek is essentially betting that the future of AI infrastructure depends on solving the energy problem. The company's timeline and technical roadmap will be crucial to watch. If Europa Fusion can demonstrate a working prototype and a path to commercial viability within the next three to five years, it could attract follow-on funding and accelerate an entire sector of fusion-focused startups. But even if the technology works, scaling manufacturing and getting regulatory approval remain enormous hurdles.

Sticking with the theme of innovation in financial infrastructure, there's a new experiment underway that could reshape how stocks are bought and sold. A company called Fairmint is planning to build a tokenized stock venue — essentially an exchange where traditional equities are represented as blockchain tokens — and they're doing it under a Securities and Exchange Commission regulatory sandbox. The platform will operate on Canton, an Ethereum-based network designed for enterprise applications. This matters because it could dramatically lower barriers to entry for smaller companies wanting to go public, reduce settlement times from the current standard of two business days to near-instant, and create new opportunities for fractional ownership. Fairmint isn't alone in exploring this space, but having explicit SEC blessing through the experimental framework is significant. It signals that regulators are genuinely exploring how blockchain technology might improve capital markets infrastructure. The experiment will run for a defined period with specific participant limits, allowing the SEC to gather data on whether tokenized trading actually delivers the efficiency gains supporters claim. The real test will come when — or if — this framework expands beyond the sandbox. If it works, you could see traditional exchanges integrating blockchain-based settlement within the next five years. If it doesn't, it could reinforce the view that tokenization is a solution in search of a problem.

Now, turning to something that will make mathematics professors everywhere either celebrate or question their life choices: artificial intelligence has potentially solved the Riemann hypothesis, a problem that's stumped mathematicians for a hundred and sixty-seven years. At the same time, a human researcher has apparently solved it too. The Riemann hypothesis is one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics — proving or disproving it would be absolutely groundbreaking and would reshape our understanding of prime numbers and their distribution. An AI system recently presented what appears to be a valid solution. Independently, a human mathematician also submitted what they claim is a proof. The mathematics community is currently in the process of reviewing both proofs, which is a meticulous and slow process. What's remarkable here is that neither proof has been universally accepted yet. Proving a proof is correct to the standards required by the mathematics community can take months or years. What matters about this race is that it highlights the evolving relationship between human and artificial intelligence in pure mathematics. AI can rapidly explore enormous solution spaces and spot patterns that might take humans years to notice. But human mathematicians bring intuition, creativity, and the ability to construct genuinely novel approaches. The outlook is particularly interesting. If either the AI or human solution holds up to scrutiny, it represents a monumental moment in mathematics. If both fall short, it'll be a valuable reminder that even with modern computational power, some problems remain genuinely difficult.

There's an important development happening in Hong Kong's approach to cryptocurrency regulation. Hong Kong is moving to extend its crypto licensing requirements to brokers, advisers, and custodians — essentially bringing the entire ecosystem of crypto service providers under regulatory oversight. This is significant because Hong Kong has been positioning itself as a major financial hub for digital assets, particularly after mainland China essentially shut down most crypto activity. By extending licensing rules to these service provider categories, Hong Kong is trying to create a more comprehensive regulatory framework that can protect consumers while still allowing innovation. This applies to anyone who wants to provide brokerage services, give investment advice on crypto, or hold crypto assets in custody. The move suggests Hong Kong regulators believe that having clear rules around these services is essential for the market to mature and for institutions to feel comfortable participating. It matters because other jurisdictions are watching how Hong Kong structures these rules. If the framework works well, it could become a template for other Asian financial centers. If it's too restrictive and stifles innovation, other jurisdictions might take a different approach. The impact will be felt most immediately by companies operating in Hong Kong or trying to expand into the market. They'll need to either obtain these new licenses or restructure how they serve Hong Kong clients.

There's a serious data security incident that's come to light involving a transcription company that works with law enforcement and major technology companies. A simple security lapse exposed sensitive customer data online without any password protection. The company provides transcription services that are used by police departments to transcribe interviews and bodycam footage, by prosecutors in preparing cases, and by major tech companies in various applications. Because it's a service provider for law enforcement, the data exposed includes transcriptions of police interviews and potentially sensitive information from active or closed cases. This is exactly the kind of incident that creates real problems on multiple levels — it's a privacy violation for individuals whose statements or images were recorded, it's a security risk for ongoing investigations, and it reflects poor security practices at a company handling some of the most sensitive information in the system. The company apparently had data stored on a cloud server with no authentication required, which means anyone who stumbled upon the URL could access everything. What matters here is what happens next. The company needs to notify all affected parties, law enforcement needs to assess what information was exposed and whether any cases are compromised, and there need to be real consequences — both financial and reputational — for this kind of negligence. This kind of incident should trigger a broader audit of how transcription and other service providers are protecting sensitive data. As more critical infrastructure and sensitive information moves to cloud services, we need to see much higher standards than what this company apparently had in place.

Insurance has become a quietly solid place to park money, with a number of insurance companies posting strong returns in recent months. Part of what's driving this is simply that insurance stocks had been undervalued for a while, so when they finally got some recognition, they popped. But there's also the broader factor that insurance companies generate steady cash flows and don't have the same high-multiple, high-growth expectations that tech stocks do. In an environment where interest rates have been elevated and macro uncertainty has been high, that consistency has real value. Insurance stocks have also benefited from improving underwriting results as rates have hardened across the industry. The sector has also seen some consolidation activity and strategic moves that have created value for shareholders. What's interesting about this observation is that it highlights how different parts of the market move in cycles. When investors got excited about artificial intelligence and tech stocks earlier in the year, insurance and other traditionally defensive sectors underperformed. As sentiment has shifted and concerns about valuations have grown, those more boring sectors have come into their own. The outlook for insurance stocks depends partly on interest rate expectations and partly on how the economy performs. If we head into a period of economic slowdown, insurance underwriting could get more competitive and pricing power could diminish. If the economy stays resilient, insurance companies should continue benefiting from pricing discipline and strong investment returns.

Zurich Insurance Group, one of the world's largest insurance companies, held an analyst and investor day, and the company shared its strategic outlook and financial performance. Zurich is focusing on improving profitability through better underwriting discipline, investing in digital capabilities, and strategically expanding in markets where it sees growth opportunities. The company has been managing through a challenging period for insurance — with elevated claims costs, rising reinsurance expenses, and changing risk profiles due to climate factors. Zurich's strategy emphasizes disciplined pricing, focusing on profitable segments rather than chasing market share through lower premiums. The company is also investing significantly in digital and data capabilities, which is becoming increasingly important for insurance companies to price risk accurately. What's notable is that Zurich, like much of the insurance industry, is emphasizing that sustainable profitability requires being selective about which business to write and at what prices. This is a marked shift from periods when insurance companies would write business at unprofitable rates just to maintain market share. For shareholders, the key will be whether Zurich can deliver on its profitability targets while growing the top line. The company will also need to navigate increasing pressure from climate-related losses and the need to continuously adapt its underwriting models. Watch for quarterly results over the coming quarters to see if the company is actually executing on its strategic plan.

Here's a challenge that's starting to get real attention in the space sector: when companies want to launch satellites and space-based infrastructure, how do lenders feel about their collateral getting shot into orbit? This is a surprisingly practical problem for SpaceX and other space companies looking to build space data centers or other on-orbit infrastructure. Traditional lenders are accustomed to collateral they can touch, monitor, and potentially recover if a loan goes bad. When equipment is in orbit, all of that becomes much more complicated. If a space data center or satellite stops working, lenders can't exactly repossess it. The space industry is growing — there's real interest in everything from satellite-based computing to in-orbit manufacturing to space tourism infrastructure. But financing these ventures requires finding lenders willing to accept the unique risks of space-based collateral. This matters because it's one of the practical hurdles that space infrastructure companies will need to overcome to scale. Some lenders are developing new frameworks for space lending, including insurance products that protect against loss or damage. Others are simply charging higher interest rates to compensate for the risk. What we're watching for is whether specialized space lending markets develop, or whether space companies continue struggling to access capital on reasonable terms. If specialized lenders and insurance products mature, it could unlock billions in investment for space infrastructure. If they don't, it'll remain a financing bottleneck for companies trying to build meaningful on-orbit assets.
