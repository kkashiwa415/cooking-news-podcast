# Morning News Briefing – Thursday, November 26, 2026

Nvidia's governance troubles just went from internal headache to credit agency concern, and it could get expensive real fast.

The rating agency is signaling a potential downgrade of Nvidia's credit rating to AA minus, which would be the fourth-highest available. The move hinges on two major worries: corporate governance issues and what they're calling key-man risk. That's banker speak for the company being too dependent on one person. In Nvidia's case, that's founder and CEO Jensen Huang. The concern is that if something happened to Huang or if he stepped away, the company's leadership structure might not be able to weather the transition smoothly. When a credit agency starts talking about key-man risk at a nearly two-trillion-dollar company, it's not just theoretical handwringing. A downgrade could increase Nvidia's borrowing costs and make it more expensive to finance operations and acquisitions going forward. The governance piece stings even more when you consider that Huang himself has been accused of breaking the company's own code of conduct. His own employees might be watching to see how the company handles that situation, and a credit downgrade could reinforce the perception that there's instability at the top.

In other news, Europe's crypto landscape just got a whole lot clearer. The French Senate blocked a new cryptocurrency tax proposal along with a contentious wallet registry that critics had warned posed serious security risks. Supporters of the registry claimed it was necessary for regulatory oversight, but opponents pushed back hard, arguing that publishing a list of crypto wallet holders would essentially create a kidnapping target list. The defeat of this proposal signals that at least in France, lawmakers aren't ready to sacrifice privacy and security concerns for the sake of tax collection. This matters because France has been one of Europe's more aggressive voices on crypto regulation, so a setback here suggests that even hawkish lawmakers see limits to how invasive regulation can get. For crypto users across Europe, this is a temporary win for privacy advocates, though the regulatory pressure isn't going away entirely.

Speaking of regulatory approvals, the FCC just gave SpaceX the green light to put artificial intelligence data centers in orbit. This is a genuinely significant moment for space infrastructure. SpaceX has been pitching satellite-based data centers as a way to process AI workloads in space, potentially reducing latency and energy costs compared to ground-based facilities. The FCC dismissed several key concerns raised by opponents, including potential threats to scientific research. The agency determined those concerns fell outside its regulatory authority, so it essentially kicked the problem to someone else. This opens the door for SpaceX to start building out orbital data infrastructure, which could fundamentally change how compute-intensive operations happen in the future. The outlook here is that we're likely to see more companies proposing similar space-based infrastructure projects, and the FCC's approval sets a precedent that these ventures can move forward even without fully resolving every stakeholder concern.

Turning to artificial intelligence and OpenAI specifically, the company is making a significant change to how it meters usage. OpenAI is merging its ChatGPT Chat and Work products into a single experience, and it's introducing a unified usage meter across both. What this means in practical terms is that heavy users might hit their limits sooner than they expect. If you're someone who uses ChatGPT extensively for both personal and work purposes, you'll now be drawing from the same pool of allowed usage for both activities. This consolidation makes sense from a business perspective because it simplifies the product, but it's going to surprise power users who thought they had separate allocations. OpenAI hasn't published the exact new limits yet, so there's going to be some trial and error as people figure out where the new ceiling is. The company is likely testing to see if consolidation actually reduces abuse while still keeping loyal users satisfied.

Sticking with AI for a moment, Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei is pushing back against accusations that his company is too willing to surrender human control to artificial intelligence. Rivals have criticized Anthropic and other leading AI companies for increasingly automating their own research and model development. Amodei calls these accusations absurd and says Anthropic remains firmly committed to human oversight and decision-making. This is a thornier debate than it might sound because there's a real philosophical divide in the AI industry right now about where automation should stop and human judgment should take over. Some companies are moving toward having AI systems design and improve other AI systems with minimal human intervention. Amodei and Anthropic want to position themselves as the responsible alternative, but critics argue that all major AI companies are gradually eroding human agency in the name of efficiency and speed. This tension is only going to intensify as AI capabilities grow.

Now let's talk about something with wheels. Tesla's Cybertruck, which the company announced would be discontinued, is somehow the fastest-selling used truck in America. The data is striking: Cybertruck units averaged just thirty-nine-point-one days on the market compared to sixty-five-point-five days for trucks overall. That's nearly forty percent faster, which suggests there's still enormous demand for this vehicle despite Tesla's decision to wind down production. Used Cybertrucks are commanding significant premiums because supply is artificially constrained. The discontinuation announcement actually created a feeding frenzy for existing units. This tells you that the problem with the Cybertruck wasn't the concept or the market appetite. It was likely production challenges, profitability issues, or strategic shifts at Tesla. For used truck buyers, it means Cybertruck values are likely to stay elevated for a while because new production is ending. For Tesla, it's a reminder that just because you cancel a product doesn't mean the market stops wanting it.

Turning to crypto now, and the mood among seasoned veterans in the space is shifting. Bitcoin has tumbled significantly, and longtime crypto observers are starting to say that this time actually seems different. That phrase carries real weight coming from people who've lived through multiple boom and bust cycles in crypto. The difference they're pointing to appears to be structural issues that go beyond the usual irrational exuberance followed by correction. We're seeing regulatory pressure intensifying globally, macro uncertainty persisting, and some of the narrative props that held prices up in previous cycles weakening. For crypto investors, this is a signal that the usual pattern of "buy the dip" might not work this time around. The outlook is for continued volatility and potentially deeper losses before any recovery stabilizes.

In European crypto regulation, the EU's banking watchdog is seeking new rules from the European Commission specifically targeting crypto firms that offer decentralized finance lending. DeFi lending has exploded as a category, and traditional regulators are struggling to fit it into existing frameworks. The banking watchdog wants the Commission to establish clear rules because current regulatory gaps create loopholes. This matters for crypto firms operating in Europe because new rules could mean higher compliance costs and potentially reduced revenue from DeFi products. For consumers using DeFi lending, it could mean either better protections or restricted access, depending on how strict the rules end up being.

Ukraine is moving in the opposite direction from many Western nations. Ukrainian lawmakers just passed a comprehensive crypto tax and regulation bill expected to bring in three hundred and thirty million dollars in revenue. The country is explicitly trying to bring crypto into its tax system rather than restrict it. For Ukraine, crypto represents a significant source of potential revenue and also demonstrates openness to blockchain technology and crypto innovation. This positions Ukraine differently from more restrictive jurisdictions and could attract crypto-focused companies and developers. The bill signals that Ukraine sees crypto as part of its economic future rather than a problem to be solved.

Looking at the global crypto story, Japan deserves more attention from Bitcoin investors than it's currently getting. Japan represents a massive pool of potential crypto capital and has historically been one of the largest retail investor markets for Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. The dynamics in Japan have been shifting, and investors who are ignoring what's happening there are missing crucial market signals. Japanese retail investors can move markets, and their appetite or disinterest in crypto affects global price action more than many Western investors realize.

Finally, there's a security issue developing at South Korean crypto exchange Bithumb. After a deeper probe into an October breach, the exchange is now warning users about phishing attacks. This suggests the breach was more serious than initially disclosed and that bad actors are using data from that breach to attempt follow-up attacks. Phishing campaigns targeting Bithumb users are likely to increase as cybercriminals try to capture credentials while the security incident is still fresh in people's minds. For Bithumb users, now is the time to be extra vigilant about login attempts, password changes, and any emails or messages claiming to be from the exchange. The broader lesson here is that exchange breaches create cascading security problems that persist long after the initial incident.
