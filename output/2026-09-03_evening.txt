Speaking of agriculture, fertilizer prices have become a flashpoint in this exact way. Farmers are acutely aware that their input costs are directly tied to fossil fuel prices, which in turn are tied to geopolitical stability in the Middle East. Fertilizer production is incredibly energy-intensive—it relies on natural gas for everything from manufacturing ammonia to transporting finished products across the globe. When fossil fuel prices spike due to Middle Eastern tensions, farmers feel it immediately in their bottom line. This isn't theoretical for them. A farmer buying fertilizer today is essentially making a bet on what energy costs will be six months from now when the crops are ready for harvest.

The impact hits everyone who eats. Food prices track fertilizer costs with a lag of about one to two seasons, so the pain farmers are experiencing now will translate to grocery store prices in the coming months. This creates a vicious cycle where higher energy costs push up food prices, which pushes up inflation expectations, which pushes up interest rates, which makes it harder for farmers to finance their operations. It's a textbook example of how energy geopolitics cascade through the entire economy.

What to watch for is any improvement in energy markets. If the bond market's signal about Iran is correct and tensions ease, we should see fertilizer prices drop within weeks, which would provide some relief to agricultural producers and potentially help moderate food price inflation heading into the winter months.

In other news, there's a growing debate about how we're actually keeping children safe online. Parent anxiety about digital harms has never been higher. Kids are spending more time in digital spaces than ever before, and the risks—from exposure to inappropriate content to cyberbullying to worse—are very real. In response, millions of parents are turning to content-monitoring apps that scan their children's texts, photos, and emails in real time. The idea is simple: if you can see what your kids are doing online, you can intervene before bad things happen.

But here's the tension. These apps create a surveillance architecture that, while well-intentioned, raises serious questions about privacy, trust, and whether constant monitoring actually makes kids safer or just shifts the problem around. When kids know they're being watched, they often become more secretive rather than more cautious. And the data these apps collect creates new privacy vulnerabilities—if an app company is collecting detailed information about millions of children's communications, that database becomes an attractive target for hackers.

The experts seem to be converging on a middle ground: monitoring tools can be useful, but they work best as part of a broader strategy that includes open communication with kids, digital literacy education, and helping them develop critical thinking about online spaces. The monitoring itself isn't the solution; it's just one tool in a much larger toolkit.

The real shift we're likely to see is toward apps and platforms that focus more on teaching kids how to navigate digital spaces safely rather than just reporting every conversation back to parents. That's a harder problem to solve than just surveilling everything, but it's probably more effective in the long run.

Turning to artificial intelligence and enterprise deployment, Fortune 500 companies are rapidly moving agentic AI from pilots into actual production systems. These are autonomous AI agents that can work independently to accomplish complex tasks—everything from analyzing customer data to managing supply chains to handling financial workflows. Eighty percent of Fortune 500 companies are already experimenting with these systems, but scaling them is the real challenge.

The problem is integration. An AI agent needs to connect to multiple systems within a company—databases, customer relationship management platforms, financial systems, collaboration tools—and coordinate with human workers and other AI agents. It also needs to do all of this while maintaining security and compliance with regulations. That's not easy. Most companies have legacy IT infrastructure from the last twenty years, and connecting new AI agents to all those systems while making sure data flows correctly and safely is genuinely difficult engineering work.

The companies succeeding at this are building what you might call AI orchestration layers—middleware that helps multiple agents work together, access the data they need safely, and operate within defined boundaries. Think of it like building air traffic control for autonomous agents instead of airplanes. We're likely to see a wave of specialized software companies emerge to solve this integration problem, because it's becoming clear that this is where the real value in enterprise AI deployment lives.

If CME wins and perpetual futures fall under their jurisdiction, it could change how these contracts are regulated. If the CFTC wins, their approach continues. What's likely to happen is that a judge will rule on the specific legal question, but this dispute will probably continue in different forms because the underlying ambiguity about which agency has authority over cryptocurrency derivatives hasn't really been resolved by Congress.
