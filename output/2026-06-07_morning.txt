The biggest AI companies in the world are about to go public, and that could reshape the entire market.

Let's start with what's happening in the tech world right now. Major artificial intelligence companies are gearing up for initial public offerings, and when they hit the market, it's going to create massive demand for the tokens and assets they use. We're talking about the computational backbone of modern AI—the infrastructure that powers everything from language models to image generation. When these companies list on public exchanges, institutional investors and retail buyers will suddenly want exposure to the sector, and that typically drives prices up significantly. The thing is, this isn't just speculation. These AI companies need enormous amounts of compute power, and they're increasingly tokenizing their infrastructure and assets. So you're looking at a potential influx of capital flowing into a relatively limited supply of assets. That's the classic setup for substantial price appreciation.

Who does this affect? Well, directly it affects anyone holding tokens or cryptocurrency assets tied to AI infrastructure. It affects existing investors in smaller AI companies who might see acquisition interest rise. But more broadly, it affects anyone with a portfolio, because major capital shifts like this don't stay isolated in one sector. When billions of dollars start moving into tokens and digital assets, it changes the broader investment landscape. The timing also matters—we're seeing this convergence of institutional money entering crypto at the same time that AI companies are preparing to go public. That's a powerful combination.

Why does this matter? Because for years, crypto and traditional markets operated in separate silos. Now those barriers are coming down. When a major tech company goes public and suddenly needs access to tokenized assets, that legitimizes the entire sector in the eyes of institutions. We could be looking at a structural shift in how money flows through financial markets.

In other news, Congress is working on significant changes to how cryptocurrency is taxed. The Ways and Means Committee has been reviewing several proposals that would reshape how the IRS treats digital assets. The details vary depending on which bill you're looking at, but the overall direction is clear—they're trying to create a more coherent tax framework for crypto transactions. Right now, the tax treatment of crypto is inconsistent and confusing, which creates problems for both regular users and institutional players who want to operate within the rules but aren't always sure what the rules actually are. These new bills are attempting to fix that, though naturally, different groups in the crypto community have different views on whether the proposed changes are improvements or problems. The key thing to watch is whether these bills gain traction and what final form they take. Congress moves slowly on technical issues like this, but the fact that serious legislative attention is happening suggests we're moving toward more formal regulation rather than continued ambiguity.

Turning to crypto markets specifically, we're seeing some dramatic moves right now. Bitcoin traded near sixty thousand dollars this week, which is a stark contrast to where things stood back in February. What's changed is institutional sentiment. Back at the start of the year, there was genuine skepticism about whether crypto could recover from the damage of past cycles. Now, big money is viewing digital assets completely differently. Institutions are getting comfortable with crypto the way they're comfortable with any other asset class—as something that deserves a place in a diversified portfolio. That shift in perception is powerful. It doesn't guarantee prices keep going up, but it does suggest that the foundation of demand has changed from pure retail speculation to something more durable. As long as institutions keep the bid under the market, you're likely to see better price support than you did in previous cycles.

But here's the other side of that coin—crypto just had one of its worst weekly performances since the FTX collapse. Bitcoin and ether shed massive value this past week as the market shed nearly four hundred billion dollars in total crypto market capitalization. That's the kind of volatility that reminds everyone why crypto remains risky. The recovery in sentiment is real, but so is the reality that these assets are still prone to sudden, violent moves. So we're in this interesting moment where institutional confidence is growing, prices have recovered significantly, but the underlying volatility and risk profile haven't gone away. That's why crypto remains a speculative asset class even as it becomes more mainstream.

Speaking of tokenization, there's growing momentum around the idea that Wall Street's next big crypto opportunity isn't just trading digital currencies—it's tokenizing traditional financial assets. The argument goes like this: instead of holding a stock certificate or a bond, what if those assets existed on a blockchain? You get transparency, faster settlement, twenty-four seven trading, and easier access for investors globally. It sounds theoretical, but companies are seriously working on this infrastructure. What started as a fringe idea in the crypto community is becoming something that traditional finance is taking seriously. Banks, brokers, and asset managers are exploring how they could tokenize equities, bonds, commodities, and other instruments. The potential is massive because there are trillions of dollars in traditional assets sitting outside the crypto ecosystem. If even a small percentage of that gets tokenized over the next five to ten years, it transforms what crypto becomes. Instead of being a separate asset class, it becomes the underlying infrastructure for traditional finance.

Now let's shift gears to some major security news. This year has seen some of the worst hacks and data breaches we've experienced in a very long time. We're talking about a massive breach affecting government records, the hacking of critical energy and water infrastructure systems, and the compromise of an FBI surveillance system. These aren't small incidents—they're the kind of breaches that get Congress's attention and trigger investigations. The DOGE data breach alone affected millions of records. Critical infrastructure hacks are particularly concerning because they're not just about stolen information; they represent vulnerabilities in systems that millions of people depend on. An energy or water system hack could have real consequences for public safety. And when you have an FBI surveillance system compromised, that's a counterintelligence concern. What these breaches tell us is that even high-security systems with significant resources behind them are vulnerable to sophisticated attackers. The attackers behind these incidents are likely state-sponsored groups or highly organized criminal enterprises with serious capability. This has implications for how we think about cybersecurity investments, about the skills shortage in security teams, and about whether we're even asking companies and governments to secure systems in ways that are actually feasible. This is going to be a major conversation for the rest of the year.

On the hardware side, Apple is holding its Worldwide Developers Conference coming up soon, and there's real anticipation about what they're going to announce. The big one is a complete revamp of Siri. Siri has been around for over a decade now, and while it works, it's never really competed effectively with the newer AI assistants that have emerged in recent years. Apple knows this. They've been working on a major update that's supposed to make Siri more intelligent, more capable, and more integrated into iOS and macOS. They're also expected to announce updates to Apple Intelligence, which is their suite of on-device AI features. The thing that matters here is that Apple has been careful about how it implements AI. They've emphasized on-device processing and privacy, which is a different approach than some competitors who emphasize cloud-based AI. If the Siri revamp and Apple Intelligence updates actually deliver on that promise—better AI that respects privacy—it could be a significant competitive advantage. Privacy is increasingly something consumers care about, and Apple is betting that they can build AI capabilities while maintaining that differentiation. What to watch for at WWDC is whether the demos are genuinely impressive or whether this is mostly incremental improvements to features people already have.

Over at OpenAI, they're continuing work on what's being called a super app. One senior executive at the company stated flatly that chat is dead. What they mean by that is that the era where you went to a separate chat interface to interact with AI is ending. Instead, they're building something much broader—an application that integrates AI into everyday tools and workflows. Chat becomes just one mode of interaction rather than the main event. Think about it: instead of going to ChatGPT to ask a question, what if AI assistance was built into your email, your calendar, your browser, your documents? That's the direction OpenAI is heading. This represents a strategic shift from being a specialty tool to being infrastructure that powers many different applications. The challenge is that building a super app is incredibly difficult. You need to integrate with other services, you need to maintain consistent user experience across different contexts, and you need to handle the security and privacy implications of being embedded so deeply into users' workflows. But if OpenAI pulls it off, it positions them differently than they're positioned now. They become less like a search alternative and more like an operating system layer.

On the investment side, Toronto-Dominion Bank is presenting an interesting technical picture right now. The bank's fundamentals are what they are—earnings, balance sheet, all the traditional metrics you'd look at for a financial institution. But the chart is doing something different. The price action is suggesting something more optimistic than what the fundamental data alone would predict. This is the classic case of technicals defying fundamentals. Sometimes that means the market knows something that isn't reflected in the latest earnings report yet. Sometimes it means the market is getting ahead of itself. With a large bank like TD, you have to remember that these are defensive holdings for a lot of investors. They pay dividends, they're essential to the economy, and they're not going away. So even when fundamentals look challenged, investor interest in the sector can keep prices supported. That said, anything can change, and a true deterioration in the economy would test how well technicals can hold up against fundamentals. This is one to watch for signs of whether the technical strength is sustainable.

Storage and real estate are rarely the sexiest topics in finance, but there's something interesting happening in the self-storage sector. The commodity of empty space—literal physical storage space—continues to be valuable. Storage companies have done well over the years because consumer behavior has shifted toward accumulating more stuff and needing somewhere to put it. The business is resilient because it serves both individual consumers who need somewhere to store furniture when they downsize or move, and small businesses that need space without committing to full warehouses. Self-storage has some nice characteristics from an investment perspective: it's relatively defensive, it has pricing power because the product is specific to location, and it generates steady, predictable cash flows. The sector has delivered solid returns, and there's no obvious reason that changes in the near term. Consumer behavior around consumption and storage isn't shifting, and demand for well-located storage remains strong.

There's also this broader dynamic happening in the market around stocks that have significant competitive moats. Companies with durable competitive advantages—whether through network effects, switching costs, regulatory barriers, or unique technology—are seeing growing investor interest. Some of this is driven by the expanding footprint of AI. When companies add AI capabilities to their core business, they can defend and expand their moats. A company that already has a large customer base can integrate AI in ways that strengthen their competitive position. Investors are increasingly valuing that kind of durability, especially in a market that's been shaped by disruptive tech for the past fifteen years. The idea is that if you own companies with real competitive advantages, you have protection against disruption. That's attracting capital, and it's lifting valuations in certain pockets of the market. The risk, of course, is that new disruptive competitors can still emerge, and past advantage isn't always a guarantee of future success.

Finally, there's a big conversation happening about what's next for SpaceX. The company has been growing its commercial satellite internet business, its government contracts, and its lunar and Mars ambitions. There's been speculation for years about whether SpaceX might go public, and those conversations are heating up again. An IPO would give the company access to public capital markets, which would fund ambitious projects like Starship development and Mars missions at a much larger scale. From an investor perspective, SpaceX represents exposure to multiple growth trends—space infrastructure, satellite internet, government space spending—all under one roof. The economics of the business are improving as launch costs come down and commercial demand grows. If SpaceX does decide to pursue an IPO in the near term, it would be one of the most significant tech offerings in years. The company's valuation would be worth watching closely because it would set a benchmark for how the market values space infrastructure companies going forward. Whether it happens this year, next year, or further out, the fact that it's being seriously discussed suggests we're moving closer to seeing a major space company with public shareholders.
