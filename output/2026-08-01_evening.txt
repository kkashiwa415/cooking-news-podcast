Snapchat just took a major stand against artificial intelligence drowning out real creators.

The platform has overhauled how it recommends videos in its Spotlight feature, and here's the key change: content that's entirely generated by AI is no longer eligible for those recommendation boosts. This is Snapchat drawing a line in the sand against what's become known as AI slop—the flood of low-effort, algorithmically generated content that's been degrading user experience across social platforms.

The timing matters here. We're in a moment where generative AI tools have become so accessible and so cheap to run that you can basically flood the internet with mediocre content at scale. Snapchat's Spotlight feature had been particularly vulnerable to this because it's a discovery-driven feed similar to TikTok, which means the recommendation algorithm is king. And when you can generate hundreds of videos for nothing, the incentive to game that system becomes enormous.

The people affected here are creators—real human creators—who depend on algorithmic amplification to build audiences and earn money from their work. Snapchat's also protecting its users from a progressively worse experience where finding authentic content becomes harder and harder. And frankly, the platform itself has a vested interest in keeping Spotlight valuable as a feature that drives engagement.

Why this matters is that it's a direct rejection of the idea that AI-generated content should be treated the same way as human creativity. Snapchat's betting that users care about authenticity, and they're willing to enforce that preference through their recommendation systems. This could pressure other platforms to follow suit, though we'll need to watch whether this actually sticks or whether the economic incentives to promote AI content eventually win out.

Now let's turn to some stock analysis. Snapchat's parent company Snap aside, there's real momentum building in the restaurant sector that deserves your attention.

Starbucks is in the middle of a serious turnaround, and the operational improvements are becoming impossible to ignore. Same-store sales are rebounding, customer traffic is trending better, and the company's getting its operational house in order after a period of real struggle. The new leadership team has made some tough but necessary calls, and investors are starting to see the payoff in the numbers.

Here's the thing though: even as the turnaround accelerates, the stock isn't necessarily a buy at current levels. The market's already priced in a lot of the good news that's happening. The company still faces headwinds from labor costs, competitive intensity in coffee and quick-service restaurants, and consumer spending patterns that remain choppy. There's execution risk here—turnarounds can stall—and much of that positive sentiment is already reflected in the share price. If you're thinking about adding Starbucks to your portfolio, make sure you're comfortable with the risks and that you're getting an adequate margin of safety for the premium valuation.

Staying with beaten-down stocks that might offer opportunity, Evolution AB is worth looking at if you're seeking quality at a discount. This is a debt-free company with genuinely high margins—the kind of fundamentals you'd normally pay a premium for. Yet it's trading at a valuation that doesn't reflect that quality. The company's demonstrated consistent earnings growth and has the financial flexibility that comes with zero debt hanging over it. That's increasingly rare in this environment.

The appeal here is straightforward: you've got a compounder with a clean balance sheet trading at prices that suggest the market has forgotten about it or is overly worried about cyclical headwinds. The challenge is that quiet, steady companies don't tend to generate the headlines that drive share prices higher. Watch for quarterly earnings reports and any big contract wins or strategic announcements that might reignite investor interest.

Turning to energy and utilities, there's an interesting dynamic playing out at Engie. The French energy giant has actually improved its guidance, which is the kind of positive signal you want to see from a company in that sector. The business is performing better than expected, and management is confident enough to raise their outlook.

But here's where it gets complicated: improved guidance doesn't automatically mean upside from here. The earnings quality matters enormously, and when you dig into how those earnings are being generated, there are some questions about sustainability and what it might take to maintain them. The company's also operating in a heavily regulated environment where margins can be squeezed from policy changes, and energy markets remain volatile. For investors, this is the scenario where the story's getting better but the valuation already reflects much of that improvement. It's not a screaming buy despite the positive news, and the path to significantly higher stock prices requires not just maintaining these improvements but accelerating them further.

In consumer discretionary, Canada Goose presents a different kind of story. The luxury parka maker is showing resilience during what should be its tough season—summer months when nobody's buying heavy winter jackets. That off-season resilience is genuinely impressive and suggests that the brand still has cachet and customer loyalty even when it's not coat-buying season.

The valuation, though, has bumped into what looks like a floor. The stock has sold off enough that it's starting to look interesting on a purely technical basis, but there's not a lot of room for further downside before you're getting a legitimately compelling entry point. We're in neutral territory here—not cheap enough to be a clear bargain, but not so expensive that you need to avoid it. If you own the stock, hold it. If you're thinking about buying, wait for a better price or for the company to demonstrate that their off-season momentum can carry into peak selling season.

Finally, here's one more industrial story that might interest you: Modine Manufacturing has taken a beating in the selloff, and the price action is starting to create a more reasonable entry point for value-oriented investors.

Modine makes heat transfer equipment and thermal solutions, which means it's cyclical and sensitive to economic conditions. During a period of uncertainty about growth, exactly the kind of stock that gets hammered. The problem is that the selloff has been pretty indiscriminate, and Modine got caught up in broader industrial weakness even though the company's fundamentals haven't deteriorated nearly as much as the stock price suggests.

The better entry point is meaningful here because Modine isn't a cheap stock that's about to turn profitable. It's a reasonably solid industrial business that produces cash and has real competitive advantages in its markets. But it's been repriced for a much worse scenario than is likely to actually happen. For investors with conviction on the industrial cycle and the economic outlook, the recent pullback creates a genuinely better risk-reward setup than you had just a few weeks ago.
