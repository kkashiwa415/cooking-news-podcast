Grok's making movies now, and the results are exactly what you'd expect when an AI tries to tell a story without actually understanding what makes one worth telling.

The company released the first seven minutes of its AI-generated film called Odyssey, and while the technical execution is there—smooth motion, decent visual quality—something crucial is missing. There's no artistry, no real inspiration, and the human interest that makes cinema compelling just isn't present. The footage exists. It moves. It resolves. But it doesn't move you, if that distinction makes sense. This matters because it highlights a persistent gap between what AI can do technically and what it can't quite grasp creatively. As AI video generation becomes more accessible and companies race to deploy it, we're going to see a lot more of this—technically proficient content that feels hollow. Watch for how studios and creators respond. Do they start layering human curation and direction into these AI pipelines, or does the market accept a flood of slick-but-empty content? That answer probably tells us something important about where entertainment is headed.

In other news, the broader question of whether AI is actually delivering value to companies remains deeply unclear. An economist from Apollo recently analyzed corporate America's AI spending, and the findings are sobering. Despite massive investments across the economy and hype that's reached a fever pitch, the technology has delivered zero financial payoff to most companies outside the tech industry itself. This matters enormously because it suggests the current AI boom might be built partly on speculation and hope rather than proven returns. Tech companies and a handful of others have seen real gains, but insurance companies, manufacturers, financial services firms, and most traditional industries haven't moved the needle meaningfully. Why does this happen? Implementation is harder than it looks. Integrating AI into existing processes takes more time and money than many expected. Skills gaps are real. And perhaps most importantly, many companies still haven't figured out what specific problems AI actually solves for them. The stakes couldn't be higher—this affects every public company trying to justify massive capital expenditures to shareholders. If the payoff doesn't materialize soon, we could see a sharp correction in AI spending, which would reshape the entire sector.

On a related note, the hottest AI wearables are starting to hit the market, and this is where some of that consumer-facing excitement is actually materializing. There's the Muse Charm, which is a slim wearable packed with AI capabilities. There's Bee, Friend, Plaud, and several others all vying for wrist-space and pocket-space in your daily life. These devices are interesting because they're trying to make AI ambient and always-there rather than something you boot up on your phone. The idea is compelling—AI that listens, responds, and helps without demanding your full attention. But wearables have a brutal graveyard, and most of them fail. What makes this round different is that we finally have AI models small enough to run locally on these devices, which means better privacy and lower latency. That could be the actual breakthrough that makes wearables stick this time. Keep an eye on which of these devices actually see adoption beyond early adopters and tech enthusiasts. That'll tell us whether AI wearables are a genuine category or just another false start.

Turning to crypto and blockchain, there's some regulatory movement happening in Europe that could shape how digital assets develop. Latvia is targeting tokenization rules for next year, with a particular focus on commercial property. This is significant because tokenization—breaking down real-world assets into tradeable digital pieces—has been a theoretical promise in crypto for years, but practical implementation has been slow. Property is a logical starting point because it's high-value, relatively stable, and there's real demand from institutional investors to fractionalize it. If Latvia gets this right, other European nations will likely follow, and it could open a whole new market for blockchain infrastructure. The risk is that overly rigid rules could stifle the very innovation they're trying to enable.

Speaking of crypto and capital allocation, there's an interesting tension emerging between Strategy and Strive, two companies trying to balance bitcoin buying with preferred stock dividends. This is a nuanced problem. Both companies have raised capital from investors who want exposure to bitcoin but also want income through dividends. When bitcoin rises, management feels pressure to keep buying more. When it falls, investors want steady dividend payments to compensate. It's a classic clash between growth and yield, and there's no perfect answer. What happens here could set a template for how other bitcoin-holding companies manage these competing pressures.

Now let's shift back to tech and geopolitics. Starlink's service in Ukraine appears to be working normally again after a period of disruption. The reporting suggested that Elon Musk had ordered SpaceX to throttle Ukraine's Starlink access, which created significant backlash and raised questions about whether critical infrastructure should depend on any single person's decisions. The restoration of service is the good news, but the underlying issue remains. Ukraine has become heavily reliant on Starlink for everything from military communications to civilian internet. That dependency creates vulnerabilities, and this episode exposed just how exposed they really are. Going forward, watch for whether Ukraine and its Western allies accelerate development of alternative satellite networks, because betting everything on one private company is a long-term strategic risk.

Here's something that doesn't get enough attention but probably should. The internet's infrastructure is genuinely fragile, and AI is about to stress-test it in ways we're not fully prepared for. Every AI model needs to move massive amounts of data. Every inference requires computation. Every training run consumes enormous bandwidth. Our internet backbone, despite being the most important piece of global infrastructure, is held together by a lot of institutional knowledge, old equipment that keeps working because people know how to fix it, and frankly, some luck. Add the computational demands of widespread AI deployment to existing internet load, and you start to see potential failure points. This matters because a significant internet outage would be catastrophic for everything—finance, healthcare, communication, power grids. The problem is that identifying and fixing these vulnerabilities is expensive and nobody wants to pay for it until something breaks. This is one of those stories where the risk is very real but the urgency doesn't feel immediate enough to force action.

Related to this theme of AI's messy reality, there's actually a running list now of AI projects and startups that didn't make it. From Apple's repeatedly delayed Siri AI to OpenAI's messy super app launch, there's a whole graveyard of ambitious AI initiatives that either shut down or catastrophically missed expectations. This is healthy in some ways—it means the market is filtering out bad ideas—but it also reveals something important about hype cycles. Companies overpromise, investors overfund, timelines slip, and eventually reality meets expectations with a thud. The projects that failed often failed because they were too ambitious, too expensive, or tried to solve problems that AI isn't actually good at solving. The ones that succeeded tend to be narrow, focused, and solving a specific problem that's actually valuable. As we move further into the AI era, this pattern will continue and probably accelerate. The graveyard will keep growing, which means more failed bets, more capital destruction, and more cautious investors. That's actually fine—it's how markets work.

In the food delivery space, there's merger activity brewing. Instacart reportedly discussed selling itself to larger competitors, including DoorDash and Uber. This would be a significant consolidation in an already concentrated market. Instacart had a successful IPO recently, but going public doesn't mean you're safe—it just means you have different pressures. The grocery delivery space has been brutal. Margins are thin, customer acquisition is expensive, and everyone's racing toward profitability while losing money on nearly every order. For Instacart, a sale to a larger player with different revenue streams or better unit economics could make sense. For consumers, further consolidation in this market means fewer choices and potentially higher prices. Antitrust regulators would likely scrutinize any deal closely, especially a combination with Uber or DoorDash, which already dominate food delivery in many markets. Watch for whether the company remains independent or gets acquired, and at what price. That'll signal how valuable the market thinks the grocery delivery business actually is.

On the hardware side, Block is releasing a new voice model that sounds eerily human, and they're emphasizing that it can run locally on a MacBook M4 Max. This is important because privacy-conscious developers and companies might actually choose to use it instead of sending audio to cloud servers where it could be stored or misused. Local inference also means no latency—the response time is instant. Block's already in the financial services space with Square and Cash App, so a good voice model opens up possibilities for conversational interfaces for payment, financial advice, and customer support. The creepiness factor matters though. As voice AI gets more realistic, there's legitimate concern about misuse—deepfakes, scams, impersonation. Regulators are starting to notice this, and we'll probably see rules requiring disclosure when you're talking to an AI rather than a human. That's still a ways off, but it's coming.
