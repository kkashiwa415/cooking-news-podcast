The venture capitalist who called Facebook's dominance a decade ago is now saying the real money in AI won't go to the companies actually selling AI—and that might just change how every investor in the valley thinks about the next decade.

Let's start with the AI investment thesis. Chi-Hua Chien has built a career seeing what's coming before everyone else does. Back when Facebook was still finding its footing, he recognized something most people missed—that the real winners wouldn't necessarily be the platform itself, but the companies building on top of it and the ecosystem that would emerge around it. Now he's applying that same anthropological lens to artificial intelligence, and his conclusion is pretty bold. He's arguing that the companies selling AI—the ones building the big models, offering the APIs, charging for access—aren't going to be where the outsized returns happen. Instead, he believes the winners will be the companies that use AI to solve actual problems in ways that generate defensible competitive advantages. In other words, the enterprise companies that can cut costs, improve products, or open up entirely new revenue streams through AI adoption. This isn't just contrarian thinking—it's a direct challenge to the current narrative where everyone wants to own the model or the infrastructure. It suggests we might be in the middle of a massive rotation in how venture capital should be deployed, away from the infrastructure play and toward the application play.

This matters because it could reshape Silicon Valley's priorities over the next few years. If he's right, the biggest exits might not come from the AI labs but from the companies quietly using AI to dominate their industries. Watch for which funds start shifting their deployment strategy around this principle.

In other news, SpaceX just added some serious institutional credibility to its board. Roelof Botha, the former leader of Sequoia Capital, has joined the company's board of directors. This is happening just days after SpaceX went public in what's being called the largest IPO ever. Botha stepping into this role fills an existing vacancy, but the timing is significant. He's not just any venture capitalist—he's someone who shaped how venture capital thinks about moonshot companies and long-term value creation. His presence on the board suggests SpaceX is preparing for a very different phase of its existence as a public company. The company still has massive ambitions—Mars missions, Starlink expansion, the whole vision—but now it's got to answer to public shareholders with quarterly earnings expectations. Having someone with Botha's experience navigating the intersection of visionary thinking and public market realities could be crucial. Expect his influence to show up in how SpaceX communicates strategy to investors and how it manages expectations around its longer-term programs.

Turning to augmented reality, Snap just unveiled a pair of smart glasses that's generating as much conversation about the price tag as about the technology itself. The company's long-awaited AR glasses are here, but investors have reacted with skepticism, sending the stock down. The glasses are positioned as a major product, with all the cutting-edge AR capabilities you'd expect from a company that's been betting on this space for years. But the price point and the market timing have left analysts questioning whether this is the bet that's going to save Snap's business. The core issue is that Snap's been trying to evolve beyond being just a messaging app for years, and AR has always been the grand vision. Instead, the company's been dealing with currency headwinds, advertiser caution, and all the usual challenges facing social platforms. A premium, expensive product might be exactly the wrong thing to launch in an environment where consumers are already pulling back. Watch to see if Snap can actually move volume on these glasses or if this becomes a lower-volume luxury item that doesn't materially impact the business. The stock's reaction suggests investors are skeptical about the near-term path to profitability.

There's some significant movement happening in the Federal Reserve right now. Kevin Warsh, the new chairman, just presided over his first rate decision, and the Fed held rates steady. This is notable both for what happened and for what it signals about Warsh's approach. His predecessor's tenure ended with a lot of uncertainty about whether the Fed would cut, hold, or even raise rates depending on the latest inflation data. Warsh's steady hand decision suggests he's signaling continuity while the economy absorbs the impact of recent policy. What matters here is that the Fed's stance on interest rates affects everything from bond yields to tech stock valuations to cryptocurrency prices. With Warsh in charge, the market will be watching for signals about what his philosophy is on inflation, employment, and whether future cuts are coming. This decision to hold steady probably buys him some time to establish his credibility before making major moves in either direction.

Now let's move to some of the regulatory and policy dynamics shaping the tech and crypto world. Kentucky is making a play that could put it at odds with the Trump administration. The state is actively targeting prediction markets—digital platforms where people bet on the outcomes of events, from elections to sports to economic indicators. Prediction markets have become increasingly sophisticated and are starting to look like they could be viable financial infrastructure, but they sit in a regulatory gray zone. The thing is, prediction markets face significant skepticism from the Trump team, which has concerns about whether these platforms are really about forecasting or just gambling by another name. Kentucky moving ahead aggressively in this space suggests the state sees an economic opportunity and is willing to move faster than federal regulators. This could create a patchwork situation where different states take different approaches, or it could put pressure on Trump's people to clarify their stance. The outcome matters because prediction markets could become genuinely valuable infrastructure if they get regulatory clarity and investment, but they'll struggle if they get shut down.

In other policy news, world leaders at the G7 summit just raised a major concern that's been lurking in the background for a while now. French President Macron and Indian PM Modi both expressed alarm about something pretty fundamental: the possibility that the United States could unilaterally shut off access to American AI systems overnight. They want the benefits of cutting-edge American AI technology, but they don't want to be dependent on a tool that could be weaponized or withdrawn at any moment. The concern became real when an Anthropic blackout happened—a situation where access to the AI system was interrupted. That made the abstract fear concrete. This is going to shape how countries think about AI strategy. Some will accelerate building their own models. Others will push for international agreements about access and control. Some might move toward Chinese or European alternatives just to reduce their exposure to American leverage. The question for American AI companies is whether they can be strategic partners that countries trust, or whether geopolitical concerns force them to either be restricted in where they sell or face international pressure. This is probably the beginning of a long conversation about AI sovereignty and national security that's going to touch every company in this space.

Let's shift to the subscription scam problem that's becoming increasingly visible. The FTC just filed a lawsuit that reveals how sophisticated subscription app scam networks have become. What's happening is this: companies set up shell structures and use complex payment infrastructure to operate predatory subscription services across app stores. They'll charge people without consent, hide the subscription terms, make cancellation nearly impossible, and when one app gets shut down or delisted, they just pop back up under a new name with a different shell company. The reason this matters is that it shows Apple's and Google's enforcement mechanisms aren't keeping pace with how sophisticated these operators have become. The FTC lawsuit is naming names and revealing the infrastructure these networks use, which should put pressure on the app stores to actually enforce their policies. But it also highlights a fundamental problem: the app store model creates friction and barriers that make it hard for regular consumers to understand what they're buying, and that friction is exactly what scam operators exploit. Expect to see more regulatory pressure on app stores to be more aggressive about enforcement, and probably some changes to how subscription apps are disclosed and how cancellation is handled.

Turning to crypto and blockchain, there's an interesting real-world test happening around World Cup tickets. FIFA wanted to use Avalanche's blockchain technology to help curb ticket scalping at the World Cup. The idea is elegant—blockchain-based tickets that are tied to the original buyer and harder to resell at inflated prices. But here's the reality check: actually implementing this at scale in a real-world event where millions of people need to get tickets is messier than the theory suggests. There are technical challenges, user experience problems, and the whole blockchain infrastructure has to be robust enough to handle the volume. This experiment matters because it's a test case for whether blockchain technology can actually solve real-world problems beyond financial speculation. If FIFA can make this work, it proves blockchain has utility. If it becomes a headache, it reinforces the skepticism from people who think blockchain is looking for problems to solve. Watch to see how smoothly the ticket distribution actually works and whether scalping actually gets reduced.

Now let's talk about what's happening with stablecoin reserves. Fidelity, one of Wall Street's biggest names, is joining the competition to manage stablecoin reserves. This is a significant move because stablecoins have become essential infrastructure in crypto—they're the on-ramp, they're the trading pair, they're how people move money around. But someone has to actually hold the cash backing those coins. For years, this was informal and sometimes shadowy. Now it's becoming an actual business with Wall Street firms competing to be the custodian of stablecoin reserves. Fidelity's entry into this space is a validation that there's real business here and that traditional finance is taking stablecoins seriously. It also means better security and regulation around stablecoin backing, which should make the whole system safer. What's interesting is that this plays into a broader trend of Wall Street moving into crypto infrastructure. It's not about buying crypto or betting on tokens—it's about providing the plumbing that makes crypto actually work as a system.

Speaking of Coinbase, the crypto exchange is in survival mode and making a major strategic shift. The company's been building a business almost entirely on trading fees. Crypto traders buy and sell, Coinbase takes a cut, and that's been the model. But trading fees in crypto are volatile—they explode during bull markets and collapse during downturns. We're in a downturn right now, and trading volumes have fallen off a cliff. So Coinbase is actively trying to diversify away from being dependent on trading fees. They're building out services, they're getting into custody, they're offering products that generate revenue regardless of whether the market is hot or frozen. This is a necessary evolution for the business, but it also suggests Coinbase is acknowledging that pure trading fee revenue is too unstable to build a multi-billion-dollar company on. The companies that survive this crypto downturn will be the ones that have multiple revenue streams and can weather the inevitably volatile cycles.

Bitcoin layer-twos are facing some hard truths right now. These are scaling solutions built on top of Bitcoin—basically trying to move transactions off the main chain to make Bitcoin faster and cheaper. There are a bunch of projects in this space that raised money, built infrastructure, and were supposed to become a big part of Bitcoin's future. But in a bear market, trading volumes are low, users aren't moving money around as much, and the value proposition of layer-twos isn't as obvious. Some of these projects are burning through their treasuries, usage is flatlined, and the revenue model isn't there. This is a classic startup problem—the market didn't materialize as fast as expected. Watch to see which layer-two projects have enough funding and patience to survive the downturn and which ones run out of runway. The eventual winners could be valuable infrastructure, but the current crop might have been too ambitious about how quickly they'd see adoption.

Finally, let's talk about the state of AI spending in enterprise. We had a trend earlier this year called tokenmaxxing—basically, companies were pushing their employees to use AI as much as possible, to maximize the value they were getting from their AI tools. Uber reportedly blew through its annual AI budget in a few months. Meta killed its internal leaderboard that was tracking AI usage. Some companies cut their Claude licenses. The reason? The bill came due. You can't use unlimited AI without it costing real money, and when companies started looking at what they were actually getting for those costs, the ROI math didn't look as good as expected. The real question nobody's solved yet is whether AI adoption at the enterprise level actually meaningfully improves productivity or just shifts costs around. There are legitimate efficiency gains happening, but they're probably smaller and harder to measure than the hype suggested. What matters here is that enterprise AI spending could become a lot more disciplined and measurable. Companies will probably slow down, focus on the AI applications that actually show ROI, and stop the wild spend-everywhere approach. That could mean slower growth for AI companies selling to enterprises, but it also means the market matures and becomes sustainable. Expect more companies to quietly announce AI budget freezes or reallocations as they do their quarterly reviews.
