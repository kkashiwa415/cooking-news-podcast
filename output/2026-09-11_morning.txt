Artificial intelligence is eating everyone's lunch today, and now it's coming for the mathematicians.

Twenty-five leading mathematicians just signed an open letter accusing AI labs, particularly OpenAI, of threatening their intellectual work. The core complaint is that large language models are being trained on mathematical research and proofs without proper permission or compensation, essentially absorbing decades of scholarly work to power commercial systems. These aren't fringe academics either—these are some of the most respected names in the field. The mathematicians argue that AI companies are treating mathematics like a free resource to be mined rather than as protected intellectual property. What makes this particularly thorny is that math is foundational to modern AI itself, so you've got this weird circular dependency where companies are using mathematics to build AI that then devalues the work of mathematicians. It matters because it's setting a precedent for how creative and intellectual work gets treated in the age of large language models. If AI companies can freely use mathematician's work without compensation, what's to stop them from doing the same to writers, artists, and other knowledge workers? Watch for whether this escalates into actual litigation or regulatory pressure on model training practices.

Y Combinator's Garry Tan just made a call for a more robust American AI ecosystem, specifically asking smaller open-weight AI labs in the US to use distillation techniques on frontier models. For context, distillation is a process where you take a large, capable model and use it to train a smaller one, essentially transferring knowledge. What Tan's pushing for is essentially a two-tier American AI system where frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic build the biggest models, and then smaller labs take those models and distill them down into more accessible, open-weight versions. This is partially a response to China's own open-weight models gaining ground. The thinking is that if the US has a robust ecosystem of smaller, American open-weight models available to everyone, it prevents China from dominating the open-source AI space and gives American startups more leverage. It affects anyone building AI applications because they'd have access to better open-source options trained on American research. It matters because it's about national competitiveness and ensuring the US doesn't cede the open-source AI market to international competitors. Watch for whether any of the major US labs actually embrace this approach or whether they choose to keep their models proprietary to maintain competitive advantage.

Over in the crypto world, Robinhood's CEO has waded into the escalating battle over stock tokens, specifically in the context of the AMC feud. The company's arguing that individual companies shouldn't have veto power over whether stock tokens of their shares can be created and traded. This is about tokenization—the process of representing traditional assets like stocks as digital tokens on blockchain networks. AMC has been particularly vocal about wanting control over whether its stock can be tokenized, and Robinhood's pushing back by arguing that once a stock exists and trades publicly, anyone should be able to create a token version of it. The implications here are huge. If companies can't stop their stocks from being tokenized, it opens up a whole new market for trading fractional shares and composable financial products. It affects retail traders who might benefit from easier fractional ownership and institutional investors who see opportunities in tokenized securities. It matters because it's a fundamental question about property rights and market structure in the age of blockchain. The outcome of this debate could reshape how stocks are traded over the next decade. Watch for whether regulators weigh in and what stance they take on companies' rights to control tokenized versions of their shares.
