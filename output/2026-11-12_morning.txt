Jensen Huang's personal scandals are threatening to reshape one of the tech industry's most powerful companies at the worst possible moment.

The Nvidia CEO has found himself at the center of a compounding crisis that's now unfolding across multiple fronts. It started when Huang admitted to having an affair with a woman who's been accused of being a Chinese intelligence agent. That revelation alone sent shockwaves through Silicon Valley because of the national security implications—the U.S. and China are in an all-out race to develop the most advanced AI infrastructure, and Nvidia sits right at the center of that competition. Having the company's longtime leader entangled with someone connected to Chinese intelligence is exactly the kind of vulnerability that keeps government officials up at night.

But that wasn't the end of it. Just days later, new allegations emerged that Huang was involved in a separate affair with an Nvidia executive who works under him. This second accusation compounds the original scandal and raises serious questions about his judgment and the culture he's fostered within the company he founded thirty-three years ago.

The board of directors has reportedly been preparing to make a move. As early as Wednesday evening, they were considering removing Huang from his position as CEO. For a company that's become synonymous with the AI boom and whose stock has been a cornerstone of the entire tech rally, this kind of leadership vacuum at the top could be catastrophic. Investors got a taste of that nervousness when Nvidia's stock dropped on the news that an ouster was being seriously considered.

The people affected by this situation range from Nvidia's employees and shareholders who have massive amounts of wealth tied up in the company, to the broader tech ecosystem that depends on Nvidia's chips and partnerships, to U.S. government agencies concerned about national security and China's competitive position. And looking ahead, the critical thing to watch is whether the board actually moves forward with removing Huang and, if so, who they tap to replace him. That succession decision will determine whether Nvidia can navigate this crisis or whether it becomes a turning point that undermines the company's dominance in AI infrastructure.

In other tech news, Meta is dealing with its own leadership transition. Santosh Janardhan, who's been with Meta since twenty oh nine and most recently served as the company's head of infrastructure, is stepping down from his role. Janardhan has been a linchpin in building out Meta's massive data center operations and the underlying technical infrastructure that powers Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and all the company's other services. Infrastructure leadership is especially critical right now because Meta's been investing heavily in AI and competing fiercely with other tech giants over data center capacity and compute resources.

The departure matters because infrastructure executives are among the hardest positions to fill in tech. They're the people who understand how to scale systems to support billions of users, manage astronomical capex budgets, and navigate the regulatory and real-world constraints of building data centers around the world. Meta will need to move quickly to find a strong replacement, and in a market where everyone's scrambling for infrastructure talent, that won't be easy.

Turning to the data center boom itself—there's a reality check coming for the AI infrastructure buildout that everyone's been celebrating. Companies have been announcing massive data center projects with eye-watering price tags in the billions of dollars. But here's what's actually happening on the ground: many of these projects may never get built at all. The challenges are real and multiplying. Local communities are organizing resistance to data center proposals. Permitting processes are grinding along slowly. And there's a fundamental constraint that no amount of capital can immediately solve: electricity. Data centers consume enormous amounts of power, and the grid in many regions just can't support the load that's being planned.

So we're seeing a situation where companies have announced far more capacity than will realistically come online in the near term. Some projects will get shelved, others will be delayed by years, and the ones that do move forward will face continuous battles over siting, environmental concerns, and grid capacity. This means the actual AI infrastructure available over the next few years could be significantly more constrained than the announcements suggest. For companies betting on unlimited compute capacity and for investors pricing in rapid AI deployment, this is an important correction to keep in mind.

On the venture capital side, emerging managers are facing a uniquely difficult moment. Olaf Sakkers is one of the founders trying to break through what's become known as the emerging manager squeeze. For years now, the venture industry has been consolidating around a handful of mega-firms with massive funds and strong track records. That's made it exponentially harder for first-time fund managers to raise capital, even if they have good ideas and solid networks. Sakkers has been working for five years to prove that it's possible to succeed despite these headwinds. His story and others like it will test whether venture capital remains accessible to new talent or whether it's become a closed system dominated by established players. That has real consequences for founder diversity and for whether new voices can shape the direction of startups.

In cryptocurrency news, we're seeing movement on the regulatory and infrastructure front. The GENIUS Act, which provides certain protections and guarantees for digital assets and crypto platforms, is now law. But there's an important caveat emerging: those protections don't extend to decentralized finance lending. DeFi lending platforms operate in a gray area—they're not traditional custodians, they're peer-to-peer protocol networks. The legislative framework wasn't really designed with them in mind, which means DeFi participants don't get the same coverage that users of centralized platforms will receive. This creates a bifurcation in the crypto ecosystem where some activities are regulated and backstopped while others remain completely exposed.

Meanwhile, the tokenization of traditional assets is accelerating. OKX Ice, a platform focused on digital assets and blockchain-based trading, is set to debut tokenized stock trading this month in partnership with Strategy, Coinbase, and Circle. This is a big milestone because it represents a convergence of traditional finance and crypto infrastructure. Tokenized stocks give you all the benefits of blockchain settlement and twenty-four seven trading while maintaining the regulatory structure of equity markets. If this launches successfully and attracts meaningful volume, it could fundamentally change how equities are traded and settled.

There's also a fascinating story emerging around Spiko, which is now the largest tokenized fund in the world—and most people have never heard of it. Tokenized funds are a relatively new product category where traditional investment funds are structured on blockchain rails, allowing fractional ownership, easier transfer, and more efficient settlement. Spiko's rise under the radar shows there's genuine momentum in this space, even if it's not getting mainstream attention yet. As these products mature and more institutional capital flows into them, they could reshape how people invest.
