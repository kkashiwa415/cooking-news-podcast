Here's what's happening in the markets and tech world this evening.

Let's start with monetary policy, because it sets the tone for everything else. There's growing argument that the Fed has held rates too high for too long, and the economic data is starting to back that up. The case being made is that persistent inflation might finally be cooling enough to justify rate cuts, and keeping rates elevated any longer could start to unnecessarily drag down economic growth. If that argument gains traction at the Fed's next meeting, it could reshape borrowing costs across the entire economy—everything from mortgages to business loans. This matters because the financial markets have already been pricing in some expectation of relief, but if cuts actually materialize, it could unlock capital that's been sitting on the sidelines. Watch for any signals from Fed officials in the coming weeks, especially around inflation data releases.

In other news, there's a cryptocurrency story that's remarkable for how brazen and damaging it turned out to be. A man named Eli Regalado claimed that God was speaking to him directly, telling him when to buy and sell crypto. He convinced his investors—people who believed his divine connection was real—to pour money into his project, but then those same divine voices supposedly told him to dump the holdings entirely. When he did, investors lost everything. The story sounds almost unbelievable until you realize how many people were desperate enough to believe that spiritual guidance could beat market returns. What made this possible was a fundamental breakdown in due diligence. People wanted to believe in something bigger than themselves, and Regalado weaponized that desire. His investors trusted religious conviction over financial fundamentals, which is the exact opposite of what rational investing demands. This story matters because it illustrates just how vulnerable people still are to charismatic figures making outrageous claims in the crypto space, and it's a reminder that no matter how sophisticated our markets become, human psychology remains the weakest link. We'll likely see regulators take a harder look at how spiritual or religious leaders are positioning themselves as investment advisors.

The pitch is straightforward: they're moving beyond transaction-based models into recurring revenue streams, and they're layering artificial intelligence on top of their platform to add real value to healthcare providers. The healthcare billing and revenue cycle space has been ripe for disruption for years, and Waystar's pushing AI into that gap at exactly the right moment. Healthcare systems are desperate for anything that can reduce billing errors and speed up cash collection, so the demand is there. As Waystar expands its subscription offering, it's also improving margins and creating more predictable revenue for investors. Watch to see how quickly they can convert their existing customer base to these new subscription tiers.

Chime's digital banking model has proven the concept works—millions of customers prefer their straightforward, app-first approach to traditional banking. But the stock has been volatile, so the real question is whether it's worth buying at current prices or if there's a better entry point still coming. The company's fundamentals remain solid, but the valuation can swing dramatically based on investor sentiment around fintech stocks broadly. If you're considering it, patience might be rewarded.

Part of that discount is legacy skepticism about Japanese conglomerates generally, but Marubeni's business has been modernizing. The company's diversified exposure to energy, infrastructure, and trading gives it stability that more focused peers lack, yet the market hasn't fully repriced that benefit. As global energy transitions accelerate, Marubeni's positioned to benefit, but you're essentially betting on the market recognizing that value gap.

Now, here's where technology infrastructure becomes critical to everything else: the power grid can't keep up with artificial intelligence's appetite for electricity. This isn't theoretical. Back in July of this year, a transmission line fault in Ashburn, Virginia—which houses the world's largest cluster of data centers—knocked more than three gigawatts of load off the grid in seconds. Two years before that, a single failed component dropped about fifteen hundred megawatts across Virginia. What's happening is that AI data centers are concentrating massive power demands in relatively small geographic areas, and our grid architecture was never designed for that kind of localized intensity. The problem is that if a single transmission line fails or a piece of equipment malfunctions, thousands of megawatts can vanish from the grid instantly. This creates cascading instability. The fix isn't simple, because you'd need to rebuild how power is distributed to these facilities, which takes years and billions of dollars. What matters here is that powering AI isn't just a procurement problem—it's an infrastructure and engineering problem that's only going to get more severe. Companies building data centers need to start thinking about distributed power solutions, backup systems, and grid-hardening investment. Regulators need to start planning for this concentration of load before we see rolling blackouts in major tech regions.

Speaking of energy infrastructure, there's serious momentum building in the US battery storage market. We're seeing record growth in energy storage capacity, and crucially, it's happening because cheap Chinese battery cells are making the economics work. Battery storage solves a huge problem for the grid: it lets us smooth out the variability of renewable energy. When solar and wind are generating, batteries can store that power, and when demand spikes or generation drops, those batteries can discharge electricity back. But here's the tension: the US battery market is booming precisely because it's tapping into inexpensive Chinese supply chains. That creates a policy problem because policymakers want to build domestic battery manufacturing to reduce China dependence, but if you price out Chinese batteries with tariffs, you slow down storage deployment and make the grid transition more expensive and slower. This is a genuine policy dilemma with no perfect answer. Watch for tension between trade policy and clean energy goals to intensify in coming quarters. Companies investing in battery infrastructure need to hedge against the possibility that tariffs or trade restrictions could suddenly change the cost calculus.

On climate engineering, there's a detailed roadmap being published about how we might actually deploy solar geoengineering—intentionally reflecting some sunlight away from Earth to counteract warming. A nonprofit has laid out what experiments, studies, and infrastructure we'd need to make informed decisions about this. Solar geoengineering is genuinely controversial because once you start it, stopping it could be catastrophic, and the effects would be globally distributed in ways that some countries might not want. That said, as the pace of climate change keeps accelerating, more researchers and policymakers are taking geoengineering seriously as a potential tool. What matters is that this roadmap is trying to shift geoengineering from pure speculation to something we might actually be able to study and evaluate objectively. It's not a commitment to deploy anything—it's a framework for building knowledge. Expect this to be contentious in climate policy circles, but also expect serious investment and research funding to start flowing toward geoengineering research programs.

Turning to crypto and digital assets, Bitcoin traders are pulling back on their bullish positions as they wait for inflation data to come out. The relationship between Bitcoin and inflation expectations is complicated—sometimes people buy Bitcoin as an inflation hedge, sometimes they sell it to raise cash if they're worried about economic slowdown. Right now there's genuine uncertainty about which way the data will cut, so traders are staying light on aggressive bets. This is the kind of caution that often precedes bigger moves in either direction. Once inflation numbers hit, we could see sharper moves in crypto markets as traders reassess their inflation outlook.

There's also an interesting thesis emerging about Bitcoin as collateral. As Bitcoin's become more accepted in traditional finance, some firms are starting to use it as collateral for loans and other transactions. When Bitcoin becomes collateral, the financial math changes significantly. You need more certainty about volatility, you need clearer valuation, and you need to think about haircuts and margin requirements. This is actually a sign of maturation—using an asset as collateral means you're treating it like a serious financial tool rather than pure speculation. But it also means regulatory scrutiny will increase, because using Bitcoin as collateral in the broader financial system could create contagion risks if Bitcoin drops sharply. Watch for debates about whether Bitcoin should be allowed to serve as systemic collateral without stricter guardrails.
