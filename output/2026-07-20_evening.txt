# Evening News Briefing — Monday, July 20, 2026

The FDA just reversed course on lettuce regulations and it could reshape how you buy produce.

Let's start with that. The Food and Drug Administration announced today that it's walking back recently implemented restrictions on lettuce imports and domestic distribution that had been in place for the past eighteen months. The rules had been designed to combat contamination risks following a series of E. coli outbreaks that affected dozens of consumers across multiple states back in 2024 and 2025. The agency had required enhanced testing protocols and traceability measures for all leafy greens entering the supply chain, which significantly increased costs for producers and distributors. But after a comprehensive safety review and improved industry practices, the FDA determined that the stricter requirements are no longer necessary. The move affects farmers, distributors, grocery chains, and ultimately anyone buying fresh lettuce at the store. What matters here is that loosening these restrictions should bring lettuce prices down and expand availability, but critics are questioning whether the agency moved too quickly or if it adequately consulted with food safety advocates. Keep an eye on whether any new contamination incidents emerge in the coming months, because that would likely trigger another round of regulatory tightening.

Turning to artificial intelligence and hiring, there's something worth paying attention to if you're job hunting. New research is showing that large language models used to screen resumes are actually more likely to develop hiring biases than human recruiters are. These AI systems are picking up biases from their training data, sure, but that's not the whole story. Scientists have discovered that language models can actually create their own biases during the screening process, independent of what they learned from historical data. This happens because these systems lack the context and nuance that humans naturally apply when evaluating candidates. They might over-weight certain keywords, penalize resume formatting quirks, or make snap judgments about career gaps in ways that disproportionately harm women, minorities, or people with non-traditional backgrounds. Job seekers everywhere are affected since more companies are using AI to filter applications before humans ever see them. It matters because if AI is genuinely worse at fair evaluation than people are, we're potentially amplifying discrimination at scale. The takeaway is to be very intentional with your resume formatting if you're applying to positions at companies that use automated screening, and watch for regulatory efforts around AI hiring practices because this issue is definitely going to attract more scrutiny in the coming months.
