Meta's artificial intelligence shopping tool is causing quite a stir right now, and depending on who you ask, it's either going to reshape retail forever or it's completely overhyped. Let's dig into what's actually happening in tech and business today.

So Meta just rolled out something called Muse, which is basically an AI assistant designed to help people shop. The company's clearly betting big on turning artificial intelligence into actual revenue, but there's legitimate debate about whether this particular tool is the game-changer it's been marketed as. The reality is more complicated than the hype suggests. What Muse does is use AI to understand what customers are looking for and then recommend products from Meta's advertising partners. It sounds great in theory—personalized shopping, better recommendations, happier customers. But here's where it gets tricky. The tool isn't necessarily proving that AI can be monetized at scale in a new way. It's really just a wrapper around existing advertising and recommendation technology that's been around for years. Yes, it uses newer AI models to do it, but the underlying business model is still fundamentally the same advertising game Meta's always played. The risk here is that investors might be seeing this as evidence of a major new revenue stream when it's really just an incremental improvement on what already exists. For Facebook and Instagram users, it could mean more relevant product suggestions, which might actually be useful. For sellers and brands, it means another channel to reach customers through Meta's platform. But for Amazon, Google Shopping, and other e-commerce players, this is worth watching because it represents another attempt by Meta to own more of the customer journey. The real question isn't whether Muse works—it probably does—but whether it's transformative enough to justify the market excitement around it. We'll know more once we see actual adoption numbers and whether merchants really shift budgets to compete on this new platform.

Speaking of changing infrastructure and new approaches, there's a fascinating development happening in Congress right now around border security. A Democratic representative from Illinois just announced plans to introduce legislation that would terminate America's virtual border wall program. This surveillance tower initiative has been controversial for a while now, with questions about effectiveness, cost, and whether the technology actually delivers on its promises. The proposal targets what's often called a digital or virtual barrier—essentially a network of surveillance towers and monitoring systems designed to track movement along the US-Mexico border. The context here is that physical border barriers have become increasingly expensive and politically contentious, so there's been a push toward technological solutions. But like a lot of high-tech government programs, it's turned out to be more complicated and less effective than initially envisioned. The push to scrap it comes amid broader questions about how much surveillance infrastructure actually improves border security versus how much it costs. Communities along the border, civil liberties advocates, and fiscal conservatives have all raised concerns for different reasons. If this legislation gains traction, it could free up billions of dollars that were allocated to this program, though that's a big if in the current political environment. What we're watching is whether this becomes a real movement or just a single legislator's proposal. But it does signal that there's growing skepticism about whether fancy technology always solves policy problems.

In other news, there's quite a bit of chatter happening this week around artificial intelligence and climate change, particularly at Climate Week in New York. World leaders have descended on Manhattan for the UN General Assembly, and simultaneously, investors, policymakers, climate advocates, and journalists are all gathered for New York Climate Week. The unavoidable topic at every panel, every fancy dinner, every conversation is artificial intelligence. It's actually pretty remarkable how completely AI has taken over the climate conversation. Just a few years ago, you'd hear about renewable energy, carbon markets, emissions reduction targets. Now every single climate discussion seems to circle back to AI—how AI can help monitor emissions, how AI can optimize energy usage, how AI models themselves consume massive amounts of electricity. There's genuine potential here. AI can be incredibly useful for climate science, for modeling complex systems, for optimizing everything from power grids to supply chains. But there's also a risk that AI becomes a kind of technology bandaid that distracts from the harder work of actually changing how we produce and consume energy. The climate community is split on this. Some see AI as an essential tool that can accelerate our response to climate change. Others worry that focusing on technological fixes lets governments and corporations avoid making harder choices about reducing consumption and transitioning away from fossil fuels. What matters is that this conversation is happening at the highest levels of government and business. How seriously these leaders take the climate implications of AI development itself could shape policy for years. Watch for whether any concrete commitments come out of Climate Week about how AI development will be regulated to account for its climate impact.

There's also a cautionary tale worth considering about AI hype in general. Over the past few months, we've seen a lot of breathless claims about what artificial intelligence can do. Anthropic announced that its Claude model was better at finding software vulnerabilities than most security experts. Then there were incidents where both OpenAI and Anthropic had their models hacked, and Meta had to disclose similar security problems. The pattern here is interesting because every time a new capability is announced, we've also had evidence that these systems have real limitations and security vulnerabilities. The underlying message is that we should be skeptical of the most grandiose claims about AI right now. Yes, these systems are powerful and improving rapidly. But they're also limited in ways that aren't always obvious, they make mistakes, and they can be exploited. Companies have obvious incentives to hype their AI capabilities because it drives investment, partnerships, and talent. Regulators, investors, and the public need to look beyond the headlines and ask harder questions about what these systems can actually do versus what vendors are claiming they can do. This matters because AI is being deployed in consequential areas—healthcare, criminal justice, hiring, financial services. If we're making decisions based on hype rather than realistic assessment of capabilities and limitations, we're going to make mistakes. The outlook here is that we'll probably see more critical examination of AI claims, which is healthy. But we're also probably going to see continued hype because the incentives for companies to oversell are really strong.

Now let's talk about some of the big tech players and how they're positioning themselves in this AI-driven world. Alphabet, which owns Google, is dealing with an interesting challenge right now. The company's AI business and its cloud computing business are kind of on different trajectories, and that divergence is masking what could be a massive opportunity. Google's Gemini AI model is one of the most advanced in the world, and the company's cloud division is growing but still competing hard against Amazon and Microsoft. The interesting part is that there's potentially a trillion dollar opportunity here if Alphabet can actually integrate these two better—if they can use their AI capabilities to make their cloud services more attractive and harder to replace. Right now, cloud customers are somewhat locked into whoever they're with because switching is expensive and complicated. If Google can layer in unique AI capabilities that make their cloud genuinely better, it could shift the competitive dynamics. The company's got the technology to do this. What it needs to do is execute better on actually getting customers to adopt these integrated solutions. If they pull this off, it could be a real game-changer for their business. If they don't, they're going to keep fighting Amazon and Microsoft for share in a business where margins are getting squeezed.

Broadcom is another company dealing with AI dynamics, but from a different angle. Broadcom makes the chips and networking equipment that power AI data centers. The company's seeing demand surge, which is great for revenue, but there's a new wrinkle. China is increasingly a risk factor in the AI story, beyond just the typical concerns about margins and competition. Broadcom sells a lot of equipment to China, and there's genuine uncertainty about what trade restrictions might come next. The US government has already implemented various export controls on advanced semiconductors to China, and there's always the possibility of more restrictions. This creates a real challenge for Broadcom because China is a significant market, and if that market becomes unavailable or severely restricted, it affects growth forecasts. At the same time, the company benefits from demand from US and allied customers who are racing to build out AI infrastructure. So Broadcom's in this interesting position of having enormous demand but facing potential geopolitical headwinds that could change the game quickly. It's worth watching how trade policy evolves because it directly impacts companies like this.

There's some genuine good news on the manufacturing front, though. The United States is actually seeing a revival in manufacturing activity, and it's happening despite some significant headwinds. There are tariffs affecting input costs, there are energy expenses rising in some areas, and there are supply chain challenges that haven't entirely gone away. But companies are still investing in bringing manufacturing back to the US or expanding capacity here. The reasons are varied. Some of it's driven by companies wanting to diversify away from China amid trade tensions. Some of it's about being closer to customers and reducing shipping costs. Some of it's driven by government incentives and subsidies for domestic production. And some of it's genuine cost considerations—labor might be more expensive in the US, but automation has made labor costs less of a determining factor than they used to be. The companies doing this are betting that there's long-term value in having production closer to home, even if short-term costs are higher. This is particularly true in sectors like semiconductors, advanced manufacturing, and clean energy equipment. The challenge is whether this trend holds if trade policy changes or if the cost calculus shifts again. But for now, it's one of the few economic trends that's actually positive and worth paying attention to. The outlook is that this manufacturing revival could create jobs in communities that have been hit hard by decades of offshoring. Whether it's enough to offset other economic headwinds remains to be seen.

Looking at the second quarter of this year, healthcare investing was mixed. Some subsectors did well, particularly companies focused on areas like oncology and rare diseases where there's less price pressure. Other areas, like traditional primary care and commoditized services, faced more headwinds. We're also seeing increased consolidation as larger healthcare companies buy up smaller innovation-focused firms. The valuation picture in healthcare is complex right now because you've got some companies trading at very high multiples based on AI potential, while others are cheap because investors have given up on them. The outlook for healthcare investing is probably going to depend a lot on which bets on AI actually pay off. Some of the hype around AI in healthcare will probably deflate when we see the reality of adoption timelines and regulatory hurdles. Fuel costs, labor costs, competitive pressures from low-cost carriers, and the post-pandemic reality of shifting travel patterns. The airline industry never returned to pre-pandemic profitability levels for most carriers, and they're still working through structural challenges. Air New Zealand specifically is dealing with being a smaller carrier operating in a region with limited competition, which gives them some pricing power but also limits their ability to spread fixed costs across a huge network. The airline's recent earnings and commentary suggest they're managing reasonably well, but there's underlying tension between wanting to raise prices and fares to improve margins and the risk that they price themselves out of the market. International travel has recovered, which is good for them. But there are also new threats emerging—more airlines offering premium economy, more competition on key routes, and the ever-present threat of economic slowdown that hits travel demand hard. For Air New Zealand specifically, the watch is whether they can maintain pricing power and capacity discipline while still growing traffic enough to drive absolute profit growth. In the broader context, the airline industry is probably cyclical, and with economic uncertainty, we're probably in the later stages of this recovery cycle.

Now let's talk about Meta and Amazon, because there's actually an important reframing happening around Meta's Muse tool that's worth your attention. When Muse was first announced, the market had a pretty straightforward reaction—Amazon's shopping ecosystem could be threatened if Facebook and Instagram become viable places for people to actually discover and buy products. If Muse actually works—and there's no reason to think it won't—then Meta's effectively creating a new shopping channel with instant access to a massive customer base. Amazon, meanwhile, has built its shopping dominance through decades of network effects, trust, and infrastructure. But Amazon's advertising business has become increasingly important to profits, and they're vulnerable if customers shift to shopping on other platforms. The argument being made is that the market is actually underestimating the threat here. If even a modest percentage of shopping searches migrate from Google and Amazon to Meta, that could be huge for Meta's advertising business and costly for Amazon. This has led some analysts to upgrade their ratings on Amazon, not because Amazon's doing great, but because Amazon needs to respond aggressively to this threat, and that response could actually unlock shareholder value. Whether that thesis is correct depends on whether Muse gains real adoption and whether Amazon's leadership can actually execute a response effectively. But it's one of those strategic dynamics that's worth watching because it could reshape how people shop online.
