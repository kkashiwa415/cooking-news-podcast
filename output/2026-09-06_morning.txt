Apple's App Store just lost one of its most powerful executives, and the reason reveals a philosophical divide at the company over where billions in revenue should come from next.

Phil Schiller, who's been steering Apple's App Store for years, is stepping away from the role. He didn't leave quietly though. Behind the scenes, Schiller had serious reservations about the direction incoming CEO John Ternus wants to take things. Specifically, Ternus is pushing hard to bring in more recurring revenue from the App Store—think subscriptions, in-app purchases, and ongoing monetization beyond the traditional upfront app price model. Schiller apparently wasn't comfortable with how aggressive that shift might become.

This matters because the App Store is one of Apple's most profitable divisions. It's a closed ecosystem that generates enormous margins, and how the company chooses to squeeze more revenue from it will shape the experience for millions of developers and billions of users worldwide. If Apple leans harder into recurring revenue models, we could see more aggressive prompts for subscriptions, higher commission rates on those deals, or pressure on free apps to convert users into paying customers. Developers are watching closely because their ability to make money on iOS depends entirely on Apple's rules. And consumers will feel it in how apps evolve—whether they become more or less friendly to free usage.

The outlook here is that Ternus is signaling a more aggressive monetization posture than his predecessor was comfortable with. Watch for announcements about new App Store policies in the coming months. If Schiller's exit signals a philosophical shift, we should expect to see some pretty significant changes to how Apple runs that marketplace.

Turning to cryptocurrency and finance, there's a notable development in bitcoin-backed mortgages. Better and Coinbase have launched a product that allows borrowers to use bitcoin as collateral for mortgages, with an interesting twist: borrowers can reuse that same collateral in multiple financial arrangements simultaneously. This is actually a fairly sophisticated financial engineering solution to a problem that's been plaguing crypto-backed lending.

Here's the issue it solves: if you're a bitcoin holder who wants a mortgage, lenders want collateral. Bitcoin is volatile, so they'd typically discount its value heavily or require you to set it aside exclusively for the mortgage. That means your bitcoin is locked up and can't be used for anything else. The new structure from Better and Coinbase allows the same bitcoin to be pledged for multiple purposes, which increases its utility and makes it more attractive to borrow against.

This affects crypto-wealthy individuals who want to access credit without selling their bitcoin—people who believe bitcoin will appreciate and don't want to incur capital gains taxes. It also reflects the maturing of crypto financial infrastructure. When bitcoin lending started, products were crude and inefficient. Now we're seeing sophisticated financial products that treat bitcoin more like a legitimate asset class.

The implications are broader than just mortgages. If you can reuse collateral across multiple products, crypto becomes more integrated with traditional finance. Wealth held in bitcoin becomes more productive and fungible. However, this also introduces complexity and risk—if bitcoin crashes, the borrower might face calls across multiple financial instruments simultaneously, creating cascading liquidations.

Watch for other lenders to offer similar products and for the total value of bitcoin collateral outstanding to grow. This is still a niche product, but it's the kind of financial innovation that tends to accelerate once the first mover proves it works.

On the international finance front, there's important research coming out of the Bank of Korea about dollar-backed stablecoins. They've found that widespread use of these stablecoins—cryptocurrency tokens pegged to the US dollar—can actually push down the value of local currencies like the Korean won. This has significant implications for monetary policy and financial sovereignty.

Here's the mechanism: if you're in Korea and you can hold stablecoins instead of won, you reduce demand for the local currency. If that happens at scale across multiple countries, central banks lose control over their own money supply and inflation dynamics. It's a form of financial dollarization, but through cryptocurrency rather than traditional channels.

This research matters because it provides concrete evidence for a concern that central banks worldwide have been discussing in private. If stablecoins become ubiquitous, they could effectively undermine monetary policy tools in smaller economies. It's not an abstract risk—it's actually happening in some markets where crypto adoption is high.

Who's affected? Citizens in countries where local currency is already unstable or mistrusted—they actually benefit from holding stablecoins because it protects their purchasing power. But central banks see it as a threat to their authority. Regulators are watching this closely and will probably tighten rules around stablecoin issuance and adoption in the coming years.

Expect central banks to increasingly push back on stablecoins and probably advocate for central bank digital currencies as an alternative. This is a long-term trend that will reshape how international finance works.
