In other news, America's courts are drowning in AI-generated lawsuits, and judges are struggling to keep up.

Federal magistrate judges across the country are now spending massive portions of their day wading through litigation documents that appear to be partly or wholly drafted by artificial intelligence. The flood has gotten so bad that some courts are seeing self-represented litigants filing cases that are technically nonsensical or riddled with hallucinations that no human lawyer would ever submit. This is happening because AI has become accessible and cheap enough that people without legal resources can use it to draft complaints, even if the results are often problematic. The people affected are everyone in the judicial system: the judges themselves who have to parse through garbage, the defendants being sued in questionable cases, and ironically the actual litigants who think they're getting legal help but aren't. What matters here is that judicial efficiency is suffering at a time when courts are already overloaded. A single judge might be burning hours on a case that has no legal merit because an AI spun out something that sounded vaguely like a lawsuit. Courts are starting to implement new screening procedures and some are requiring AI-drafted filings to be clearly labeled, but there's no consensus yet on how to manage this sustainably. Expect to see more formalized rules emerging from bar associations and courts about AI use in legal documents.
