Google just froze its entire open source bug bounty program because it's drowning in AI-generated garbage.

The company announced the freeze this past week, citing a significant rise in AI submissions overwhelming their security review process. What started as a way to crowdsource security vulnerabilities from the global developer community has turned into a deluge of low-quality, machine-generated reports that waste reviewers' time and money. The problem isn't hard to understand. Bug bounty platforms are attractive targets for people running AI systems to generate thousands of reports at scale, flooding the zone with submissions that sound plausible but don't actually describe real vulnerabilities. Google, which runs one of the most generous bug bounty programs in tech, simply couldn't keep up with the signal-to-noise ratio anymore.

This matters because it reveals a real tension in how the AI era is changing security work. These programs depend on human expertise to verify and validate submissions, and when that becomes impossible at scale, the whole system breaks. Other companies are watching closely to see if they'll face the same problem. Google hasn't said when or if the program will reopen, but they're clearly working on ways to filter out AI slop before it hits their reviewers. The takeaway here is that we're going to need better automated systems to detect and reject low-quality submissions, or bug bounty programs themselves might need to become invitation-only to stay functional.

In other news, there's a federal court ruling that's putting a spotlight on law enforcement surveillance technology. A federal judge has called Flock, a license plate recognition system used by police departments across the country, what amounts to indiscriminate mass surveillance. The judge ruled that a sheriff's deputy violated a woman's Fourth Amendment rights when using Flock to search for her license plate without obtaining a warrant first. This is significant because it's one of the first major court decisions to specifically push back against how these systems are being deployed in the field.

Flock has been rapidly adopted by police departments because it's effective and relatively inexpensive. Cameras capture license plates and feed them into a searchable database, which helps law enforcement locate vehicles. But the judge's ruling raises a fundamental constitutional question about whether searching those databases requires the same legal standards as traditional surveillance. The case affects anyone concerned about privacy, particularly those living in areas where police departments use these systems. What comes next will shape how police can actually use these technologies. If this decision holds up on appeal or inspires similar rulings, it could force law enforcement to get warrants before querying Flock's system, which would significantly change how they operate.

Cities are now implementing stricter regulations, licensing requirements, and operational limits to control how fast these systems can expand. Some jurisdictions are imposing caps on how many autonomous vehicles companies can deploy, requiring insurance minimums, and mandating specific routes and hours of operation.

This is affecting companies like Waymo and Cruise, which have built their business models around rapid scaling and market dominance. The regulatory pushback means their expansion plans will slow down considerably. But this matters for everyone in cities where robotaxis operate, because it's fundamentally about whether we're going to let market forces drive transportation change or whether we're going to make these decisions more carefully through the democratic process. The outlook here is that we'll see increasing fragmentation, where different cities adopt different rules, making it harder for robotaxi companies to achieve their scaling goals. That might actually be healthier for the technology in the long run, forcing companies to prove safety and integration before expanding.

In other news regarding financial markets and bond yields, there's a bear steepening trend in the Treasury yield curve that's creating what some analysts see as a bearish case for long-term Treasury bonds. Bear steepening occurs when long-term yields rise faster than short-term yields, typically signaling inflation expectations or reduced demand for longer-dated bonds. This has implications for everything from mortgage rates to investment portfolios to government borrowing costs.

The significance here is that Treasury markets are the foundation of global finance, and when yields move significantly, it ripples through every asset class. A steepening curve like this often suggests that the market is getting nervous about either inflation or future economic growth, or both. What to watch is whether this trend accelerates or reverses. If long-term rates keep climbing, you'll see real pressure on valuations across stocks and bonds. If the curve stabilizes, it suggests the market has priced in all the bad news and is ready to move forward.
