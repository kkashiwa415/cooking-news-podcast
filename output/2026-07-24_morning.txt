A brand new AI lab backed by some of the Valley's most influential names just raised an enormous fund to automate the work that's kept programmers employed for decades.

Prentis, the new artificial intelligence lab co-founded by Reid Hoffman and Marc Pincus, is in talks to raise one hundred million dollars. The lab's fundamental bet is pretty straightforward—they believe that automating routine computer tasks is going to become the dominant use case for AI, potentially even bigger than writing code itself. This matters because for years now, the conversation around AI has been dominated by large language models and their ability to generate text, write software, and answer questions. But what Prentis is banking on is something subtly different. They're looking at the massive universe of repetitive, predictable digital work that happens across businesses every single day—the tasks that don't require creative breakthroughs but do require precision, consistency, and speed. Think automating data entry across systems, orchestrating workflows, managing customer interactions, handling compliance checks. If they're right that this is the next frontier, then investors are betting that the teams who can crack it first will be sitting on an enormous market. The funding round hasn't closed yet, but the fact that it's happening signals real confidence from some heavyweight backers.

In other news, the world of artificial intelligence is expanding into personality and interaction style. Cognition, the company behind the coding agent Devin, just acquired Poke, a startup known for creating conversational AI that actually feels human in the way it engages with users. The reasoning here is straightforward—Cognition believes that how an AI assistant talks to you, how it responds, its tone and style, is becoming just as competitive an advantage as the underlying model itself. We've spent a lot of time thinking about AI capability and raw intelligence, but there's a growing realization that user experience and personality matter enormously when you're trying to build products people actually want to use. By bringing Poke's conversational expertise into Devin, Cognition is saying that their coding agent needs to feel natural, responsive, and even personable when it's working with developers. This acquisition reflects a broader shift in how AI companies are thinking about product differentiation. As the underlying models become increasingly commoditized, the teams that figure out how to make AI feel right—how to make it match the way humans actually want to interact—those are the teams that'll win.

Turning to transportation and autonomous vehicles, Waymo is reportedly exploring whether to break up with Uber. The two companies have had a partnership for several years, but their contract expires in May of twenty twenty-eight, and now there's speculation that Waymo might not renew it. The thinking here is that as Waymo's autonomous vehicle technology has matured, the company may feel confident enough to go its own route rather than rely on Uber's platform to get rides into customers' hands. From Waymo's perspective, going independent means keeping all the revenue from their rides instead of splitting it, and it gives them complete control over how their service is branded and positioned in the market. For Uber, losing Waymo would mean finding another autonomous vehicle partner or developing their own technology, which is expensive and time-consuming. What's worth watching is whether this actually happens and what either company says about it publicly. If Waymo does go independent, it could accelerate the timeline for autonomous vehicle services becoming a standalone industry rather than a feature bolted onto ride-hailing apps.

Now let's talk about cars and criminal charges. Several Volkswagen engineers have been indicted for insider trading connected to a joint venture with Rivian. The indictment, unsealed on Friday, alleges that these engineers used confidential information about the partnership between Volkswagen and Rivian to buy Rivian stock, essentially trading on information that wasn't yet public. This is a pretty serious violation because it's not just about individual greed—it involves betrayal of corporate trust and it raises questions about how well information is being protected inside these massive automotive partnerships. What makes this particularly notable is that it highlights the tensions around major automotive deals and the electric vehicle transition. Rivian and Volkswagen have a significant partnership, and the details of that collaboration are obviously valuable and sensitive. If engineers working on that partnership are able to exploit what they learn to make personal trades, that's a governance and security failure. The broader impact here is that it puts more scrutiny on how tech and automotive companies manage insider information, especially in competitive fields like EV development.

Shifting gears to social media and youth protection, Vietnam is the latest country looking to restrict social media access for children. Australia was the first to implement a ban back in late twenty twenty-five, and that move was specifically designed to protect young people from cyberbullying, addiction, and predatory behavior. Now a number of other nations are following the same path. This represents a major shift in how governments are thinking about the relationship between kids and social platforms. For years, social media companies have operated with relatively light regulation around youth protection, relying mostly on age verification systems that are easy to circumvent. But Australia's ban signaled that patience with that approach is wearing thin. Why it's happening is pretty clear—there's mounting evidence that heavy social media use among young people correlates with mental health problems, anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders. Parents are increasingly vocal about it, researchers are publishing alarming studies, and politicians are responding. Who's affected includes obvious targets like Meta, TikTok, Snap, and YouTube, all of which derive significant revenue from younger users. It also affects the young people themselves, for better or worse—they're being locked out of platforms their peers use. What to watch for is whether these bans actually stick, whether they're enforced, and whether other countries join in. If this becomes a widespread trend, it could fundamentally reshape the social media business model in regions where youth populations are largest.

A different kind of network-building is happening in Brazil, where farmers have come up with a creative workaround to access capital. Some dairy farmers have tokenized their cows to secure loans while bypassing traditional banking limits. Banks in Brazil typically have lending caps that restrict how much they'll lend against agriculture, and that constraint has pushed farmers to look for alternatives. By tokenizing their dairy cows—essentially creating digital tokens that represent ownership stakes in animals—they're tapping into decentralized finance and finding lenders willing to provide capital based on that collateral. This is a genuinely innovative use case for blockchain technology, because it's solving a real, material problem for people who produce essential goods. It's not speculative, it's not about getting rich quick—it's about farmers accessing the capital they need to operate their businesses. From the banks' perspective, this probably looks like farmers gaming the system, and there could be regulatory pushbacks. But it also demonstrates how blockchain's property rights and collateral mechanisms can work in practice when traditional finance fails to serve a population adequately. The cattle remain real assets, the debt is real, and the tokens are just a mechanism for representing ownership and securing loans. As this becomes more common, it might force traditional lenders to reconsider their restrictions.

In a much more serious geopolitical context, the European Union just hit Russia with its twenty-first sanctions package, and this one specifically targets a one hundred twenty billion dollar cryptocurrency network. This is a major escalation in how the West is using financial sanctions. For a long time, sanctions focused on traditional banking channels and wire transfers. But Russia has increasingly turned to cryptocurrency to try to circumvent sanctions and maintain financial flows. By targeting a specific one hundred twenty billion dollar crypto network, the EU is signaling that they understand the scope of the problem and they're willing to go after it directly. This matters because it's a cat-and-mouse game. Russia will likely innovate to find other routes, and the EU and allies will need to keep adapting. But it also demonstrates that crypto's claim to be outside government control is increasingly theoretical. Major powers have the ability and willingness to target crypto networks when they see fit, which has major implications for how safe people think cryptocurrency actually is as a store of value.
