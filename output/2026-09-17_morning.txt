For SpaceX, this validates the massive progress they've made in commercial spaceflight and their long-term ambitions. For Valor's investors, it means they're getting exposure to what could be a massive payday down the line, possibly through a future IPO or acquisition. The question everyone's watching now is whether SpaceX will eventually go public, and if so, how much of that early investor faith translates into actual returns.

Now here's something that touches a surprisingly contentious issue: AM radio in cars. The House of Representatives, in rare bipartisan fashion, overwhelmingly approved legislation that would require all new vehicles to include AM radio at no extra cost. This might seem quaint in an era of streaming and satellite radio, but AM radio remains the primary emergency alert system in the United States, particularly for weather emergencies and breaking news in rural areas. Car manufacturers have been quietly removing AM radios from new vehicles, arguing that streaming services have made them obsolete. But regulators and emergency management officials pushed back, noting that not everyone has reliable cellular or satellite connectivity, especially in remote areas. This legislation essentially says that automakers can't completely phase out AM radio access in new cars. It's a small thing in the grand scheme of automotive technology, but it reflects the government's commitment to keeping certain communication channels available during emergencies.

In the startup world, Pulley is calling it quits. The cap table management platform, which was backed by serious investors including General Catalyst, Stripe, and Founders Fund, announced that it's shutting down in December. Pulley was positioning itself as a Carta rival, offering startups a way to manage their cap tables and equity information. The failure highlights just how challenging it is to build a sustainable business in the administrative software space, even with backing from top-tier investors. Startups and venture firms that were using Pulley will need to migrate to other solutions, most likely Carta, which dominates this market. The broader lesson is that being well-funded and having smart investors backing you isn't always enough if the market dynamics don't work in your favor. Pulley's shutdown doesn't mean the space is broken, but it does show that network effects and market consolidation are powerful forces in enterprise software.

Now let's talk about AI safety and the question of who's watching the watchers. Both Anthropic and OpenAI are proposing to embed independent safety evaluators directly inside their research labs. The downside is that it's still a form of self-regulation, and meaningful oversight ultimately might require government involvement and statutory requirements. For now, this is a promising but imperfect step forward.
