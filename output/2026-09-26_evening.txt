Speaking of dividend investing, there's another cautionary tale worth mentioning. The ProShares S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats ETF, ticker PID, has become a popular vehicle for chasing dividend growth. But there's a critical problem: not all dividend growers are created equal. Just because a company raises its payout year after year doesn't mean it's doing so from a position of financial strength. Some are doing it unsustainably, prioritizing shareholder distributions over reinvestment or debt reduction. The ETF itself isn't screened for quality metrics like earnings growth, balance sheet health, or return on equity. That means you could be buying a portfolio that looks good on the surface but contains some genuinely weak businesses masquerading as dividend champions. Before loading up on PID or any dividend-growth focused fund, make sure you understand what's actually in there.

Finally, let's talk about something that sounds obscure but actually matters: force majeure clauses in business contracts. There's been discussion in investment circles about how force majeure language across various agreements could affect companies and supply chains in ways that aren't always obvious to equity investors. A force majeure clause basically excuses one party from performance if something beyond their control happens. But how those clauses are written, what events they cover, and how they've been tested during recent economic stress can have real implications for earnings and obligations. This is particularly relevant in the AI infrastructure and energy sectors right now, where sudden demand spikes and supply constraints are creating contractual stress points. If you're analyzing companies with significant long-term contracts, dig into the force majeure language and understand what scenarios could trigger relief for either party.
