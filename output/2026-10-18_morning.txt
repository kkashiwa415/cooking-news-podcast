# MORNING BRIEFING PODCAST SCRIPT

Benchmark's quietly having one of the best years in Silicon Valley while everyone else seems to be losing their minds.

The legendary venture firm has reshuffled its partner ranks, grown its funds, and managed to stay laser-focused on what actually matters—finding great companies and letting them build. Five partners took the stage recently to talk about their approach, and what struck observers was how refreshingly calm they seemed. No hot takes. No fear of missing out. No pressure to chase whatever's trending on Twitter. They've stuck with San Francisco despite everyone predicting the Valley's death a thousand times over, and they're backing founders the same way they always have—with patience, strategic thinking, and a willingness to be contrarian when everyone else is panicked. It's a masterclass in doing the opposite of what drives most firms crazy right now. The venture landscape is absolutely frenzied, with mega-funds trying to move faster, smaller players trying to differentiate, and LPs demanding returns yesterday. Benchmark's message? None of that matters if you're not picking the right companies and giving them room to breathe.

In other news, the cryptocurrency world is running into some friction with traditional Wall Street, and it's coming from an unexpected direction. Major tech companies like Broadcom and Verizon are pushing back against the SEC's framework for tokenized stocks, specifically objecting to listings appearing on crypto exchange OKX and the Intercontinental Exchange. The whole idea behind tokenized stocks is to bring traditional securities onto blockchain infrastructure, making them more liquid and accessible. But these corporate issuers are worried about who they're being bundled with and what kind of regulatory scrutiny might follow. This matters because the tokenized securities space has been getting real traction lately—there's genuine appetite from institutional investors and it's a natural next step for digital assets. But if major public companies are uncomfortable with how their tokens are being listed and traded, it could slow adoption significantly. Watch for how the SEC responds to this pressure and whether they modify the framework to give issuers more control over where their tokenized shares show up.

There's real momentum building in smaller cloud computing stocks right now, and analysts are noticing the breakout is substantial enough to warrant upgrades. Cloud infrastructure that doesn't come with the mega-cap price tag is attracting serious capital as enterprises look to diversify their tech stacks and reduce dependency on the big three. These smaller players—the ones that actually get efficient at serving specific use cases or vertical markets—are finding that investors suddenly see growth potential they'd overlooked. Rising interest rates typically squeeze growth stocks, but in this case, the smaller cloud players are proving they can generate returns without requiring massive capital inputs. They're also benefiting from broader AI adoption because custom infrastructure is increasingly valuable. If you're watching the cloud sector, these smaller-cap names are worth paying attention to because they're the ones most likely to have real operating leverage over the next few years.

Speaking of cultural moments, everyone's talking about the Elizabeth Holmes documentary. "You Can See Everything" had the second biggest opening weekend ever for a documentary film, which is absolutely stunning. The Theranos story remains this weird, compulsive watch—it's got everything: a charismatic founder, wild fraud claims, massive amounts of money vanishing, and the question of how nobody saw it coming despite all the red flags. Eight years after the scandal broke and the company collapsed, people are still intensely interested in understanding how it happened. The documentary appears to go deeper than previous coverage, giving it fresh angles that are keeping audiences hooked. What this tells us is that fraud documentaries have become a genre people genuinely crave—there's something about the intersection of ambition, deception, and consequences that keeps us coming back. It's a cultural phenomenon but it's also a reminder that startup culture got burned by being too credulous, and maybe that's actually a healthy adjustment.

Markets had a rough Friday, but there's interesting data suggesting the panic might not stick around. When you look at what the volatility index is doing and what Bitcoin traders are pricing in for early next week, there's a disconnect between Friday's fear and what sophisticated investors are actually betting will happen. The VIX spiked, sure—that's the mechanical response to selloffs. But options markets aren't showing the kind of hedging behavior you'd expect if people thought the decline was going to continue. Bitcoin also held up better than you'd expect if genuine systemic fear was driving markets. This is the market telling you that Friday might have been a correction rather than the start of something worse. Of course, that could all change Monday morning, and there's still plenty of economic data coming that could shift sentiment. But the smart money seems to be seeing Friday as a opportunity to buy rather than a signal to head for the exits.

Turning to utilities and bonds, there's a real debate among income investors about which approach makes the most sense as rates continue to move. The comparison is between specific funds that track utility stocks versus those focused on utility bonds, and the analysis suggests that as rates rise—which they've been doing and might continue to do—you want to be thinking carefully about your positioning. Utility bonds lose value when rates go up because their fixed coupon becomes less attractive relative to new issuances. Utility stocks, on the other hand, can actually benefit from rising rates because they can refinance their debt at higher rates and pass some of those costs to customers, while their dividend yields become more compelling. This isn't a recommendation to buy or sell anything, but it's the kind of sector-level thinking that matters when you're trying to position a portfolio for the environment we're actually in rather than the one we're hoping for.

On the transportation front, Rivian's next chapter is taking shape. The EV maker is navigating a pretty complex moment—production is ramping up, but competition is intensifying and capital is getting more expensive. What's interesting is how much AI is becoming central to their strategy, not just for the autonomous vehicle piece but for manufacturing efficiency and customer experience. Rivian's got some real technological differentiation with their platform design, but they're in the brutal middle of the EV adoption curve where you need volume to be profitable and volume requires capital. The company's basically got to prove that their premium positioning can hold value while scaling production costs down dramatically. If they can pull that off, they're positioned well because their vehicles have genuine appeal beyond just being electric. If they can't, they're vulnerable to being squeezed by both the ultra-cheap Chinese players and the premium players who already have profitability. This is going to be the defining story for legacy automotive players and well-funded EV startups over the next two years.

Crypto is quietly becoming more integrated with traditional finance infrastructure in ways that most people haven't noticed yet. Visa, Mastercard, and Stripe have been connecting cryptocurrency and traditional cash in new ways, and the result is that stablecoin payment volume jumped forty-five percent recently. Here's what's happening: people need ways to move money between crypto and traditional finance without crazy fees or volatility. These payment giants realized they could be the bridge, and that's created a completely new use case that doesn't require you to believe in crypto as a revolutionary technology. You just need to move money efficiently. Stablecoin supply growth has actually stalled a bit because the regulatory environment has gotten restrictive and people are being more cautious about which stablecoins they trust. But payment volume increasing while supply flattens tells you something important: the money that's in stablecoins is moving a lot faster because it's being used for actual transactions rather than sitting still. This is boring stuff compared to the price action and speculation, but it's where the real adoption is happening.

In the investment world, professional managers are reassessing what's working and what isn't as the third quarter wrapped up. The macro environment remains complicated—you've got inflation that won't quite die, interest rates that are finding some kind of equilibrium but aren't going back down, and earnings growth that's real but maybe not as spectacular as people hoped. Managers who've done well have generally been the ones who actually stuck with their discipline rather than chasing whatever's working this quarter. Growth investing had a big comeback, but value is still hanging in there for managers who've been patient. The real lesson from Q3 is that volatility isn't dead and it's going to continue punishing people who trade on emotion rather than analysis. You're seeing some real dispersion in performance based on how much each fund got caught up in the AI mania versus maintaining a more balanced approach.

There's an ongoing tension between technology platforms and documentary filmmakers right now, and it's showing up in some pretty aggressive content moderation. TikTok permanently banned an advertising account for a four-hour documentary about Elon Musk, which is a pretty dramatic move and suggests the platform is taking a harder line on what kind of content can be promoted. The documentary has faced resistance across multiple platforms, which tells you something about how touchy the subject matter is and how platforms are navigating politics and business at the same time. This isn't really a free speech issue because TikTok is a private platform making its own business decisions. But it does highlight how these platforms are choosing sides sometimes without fully explaining their reasoning. The bigger question is whether having all these competing documentary narratives matters if platforms keep choosing which ones can actually reach audiences. When it comes to controversial subjects, the distribution channel becomes just as important as the content itself.

Security in crypto is having a reckoning right now. Audits were supposed to be the answer to keeping crypto funds safe, but there's a growing realization that audits provide something closer to theater than actual security guarantees. Third quarter saw about one point one eight billion dollars in cryptocurrency lost to hacks and exploits, which is staggering. The issue is that audits check code at a point in time, but new vulnerabilities emerge constantly and the ecosystem is moving fast enough that yesterday's secure system can be today's target. This is creating this weird situation where auditors are getting paid to sign off on things, investors feel reassured, and then money disappears anyway. We're probably going to see the industry develop more sophisticated ongoing monitoring rather than relying on the audit-and-sign-off model. The human element matters too—lots of hacks come from operational mistakes or insider issues that an audit of the code won't catch. This is going to take time to solve, but the current system is clearly broken.

Finally, dividend investors have a broad universe of solid companies to choose from, and the list of dividend stories from the October Russell three thousand index addition has some genuinely interesting names. The benefit of thinking about dividend stocks as a category right now is that yields are actually meaningful relative to bond yields and risk-free rates, which they haven't been for years. That's attracting a new cohort of investors who got used to bonds paying nothing and are now surprised to find they can get meaningful income from equity dividends. The trick is finding companies that can actually sustain and grow their dividends through cycles rather than just paying high yields that are vulnerable to being cut. The Russell three thousand additions often include some smaller-cap names that institutional investors haven't paid much attention to but which have solid fundamentals and genuine dividend growth potential. These aren't exciting stories but they're increasingly the space where actual value-oriented investing is happening.

That's your morning briefing. We'll be back tomorrow with whatever market drama unfolds next. Stay sharp out there.
