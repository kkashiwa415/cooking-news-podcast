Now let's talk about something that affects markets more broadly. The bond market's experiencing what's called a bear steepening in long-term rates, and it's intensifying. That's a fancy way of saying that long-term interest rates are rising faster than short-term rates, which means the curve between short and long bonds is getting steeper. This is happening because investors are increasingly concerned about long-term inflation and government spending. When the long end of the yield curve rises faster, it gets more expensive for companies to borrow money for long-term projects like building new factories or infrastructure. For individual investors, it affects mortgage rates and bond prices. If you're holding bonds, rising rates mean those securities are worth less. The reason this is happening goes back to fiscal policy and inflation expectations. Governments are spending heavily, and some folks are worried about whether inflation could return. The Federal Reserve's signaling that it might not cut rates as aggressively as markets had hoped. This matters enormously because the cost of borrowing shapes everything—from how many new houses get built to whether companies can afford to expand. The bear steepening suggests that investors are pricing in a longer period of elevated rates, which could slow growth. We should watch for any signals from the Fed about their future policy path and keep tabs on unemployment numbers and inflation data to see whether the steepening eases or intensifies further.
